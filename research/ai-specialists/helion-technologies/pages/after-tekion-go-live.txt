After Tekion Go-Live: Where Dealerships Leave Money on the Table
By Erik Nachbahr
October 13, 2026 · 13 min read

Tekion optimization is the work a dealership does after go-live to close the gap between what Tekion can do and what each department uses every day, in service, parts, sales and F&I, and accounting.

Last updated: October 13, 2026

When I started Helion, I ran it on feel for a lot longer than I should have. We didn’t have real metrics, and my thing was that we didn’t need them, because I was close to all of it and I figured I already knew how we were doing. Then we grew. It fell apart on me, and what I took from that is feel doesn’t scale and metrics do, which I got to learn the expensive way.

I think about that whenever I’m talking to a dealer who’s a few months past a Tekion go-live. Everybody’s relieved, and they’ve earned it. The conversion’s behind them, deals are getting booked and repair orders are getting closed, and the general manager will tell you it’s going fine. So I ask him how he knows. He usually laughs a little, because the honest answer is that it feels fine.

And it probably is fine. What I’ve seen, though, is that the money a store leaves on the table after go-live almost never shows up as one big miss you’d catch. It’s a setting nobody went back to, and a feature that got switched on and never picked up, and a release that came out in a month when nobody had time to read about it.

That’s the part of a Tekion move almost nobody budgets for. We’ve already written up the Tekion network and hardware readiness work that comes before the install, and the IT checklist for a DMS conversion that gets you through cutover week, and the Tekion implementation timeline that puts each of those IT tasks on the calendar. This one is the after. It’s where our Tekion enablement team spends most of its time, and it’s the stage I’d argue matters most to the store’s bottom line.

What Tekion Optimization Means Once You’re Live

Tekion optimization is the ongoing review of how a dealership has Tekion’s Automotive Retail Cloud set up and how its people use it. It compares each department’s configuration and results against a benchmark, finds the settings and features that are unused or wrong, corrects them, and retrains the people who work in them.

Notice the word ongoing. I’ve said this to dealers more than once, and I’ll say it here. It’s such a titanic shift from where you were on CDK or Reynolds and Reynolds that nobody absorbs all of it on the first pass, and you need someone with you before, during, and after. Before and during get the attention because they come with a date on the calendar. After has no date. So it slides.

Why the Gap Opens After Go-Live

Tekion puts real effort into getting a store live. Its published Implementation Terms describe a standard certification program that trains “up to 16 Certified Professionals, up to 4 per department over 4 weeks,” and there’s a Tekion Virtual Academy offered as an annual service on top of that. Tekion’s got trainers and project managers who walk the store through the conversion, and the ones we’ve worked next to are good at it.

They do rely on the dealership to watch the training, to learn the software, and to keep learning it. I think that’s fair. It’s your store. But look at who’s being asked to keep learning. It’s a service manager with a full drive at 7:30 in the morning and a controller who’s three days from month-end. They mean to get to it. They just don’t.

And the software won’t sit still while they catch up, which is the reason you bought it. At the NADA Show in Las Vegas on February 5, 2026, Tekion reported over 60% ARC revenue growth for 2025 and showed off Scheduler AI and Technician AI. Four months later, at its Tekion One conference on June 16, it announced four more, which were T1 Pro, Salesperson AI, Deal Guard AI, and Accounts Payable AI. That’s genuinely impressive. It’s also a lot to ask a parts manager to keep up with on his own.

To be clear, I’m not saying Tekion’s program is missing anything. I’m saying the opposite. Tekion’s adding capabilities so quickly, and dealers are so busy, that you need somebody to help with this stuff. Then there’s turnover. Sixteen certified people is a strong start, but count how many of them are still in the same chair two years later, and ask who trained the people who replaced them. Most stores can’t say.

Department by Department, Where the Money Sits

Some scale first. NADA’s 2025 full-year data has the country’s 16,990 franchised dealers writing more than 276 million repair orders, with service and parts sales exceeding $164 billion. At that kind of volume, a small miss repeated on every ticket turns into real money. Nobody notices it, because nothing broke.

Here’s where I’d look in each department, and the question I’d put to the manager who runs it.

DEPARTMENT	WHERE VALUE TENDS TO SIT UNUSED	THE QUESTION FOR THE MANAGER
Service	Online and after-hours scheduling, customer updates by text, digital inspections, follow-up on declined work	How many appointments last month were booked without a phone call?
Parts	Pricing and stocking rules carried over from the old DMS, special-order tracking	Who last reviewed the pricing setup, and when?
Sales and F&I	The digital deal workflow, e-signature, document checks before a deal is submitted	Which steps of a deal still happen on paper, and why?
Accounting	Invoice handling, financial statement mapping, the reporting the office still rebuilds by hand	What does the office still key twice?

Service gets the most room here, because that’s where the customer feels it. The Cox Automotive Service Industry Study, published in November 2025, found that only 54% of people with cars two years old or newer went back to the dealership where they bought for service, down from 72% in 2023. Nearly half of owners told Cox they were dissatisfied with dealership service, mostly over unexpected costs and poor communication. Read that again. Poor communication. The store bought a platform that can text the customer, send the inspection, and take a payment, and whether any of that happens comes down to how the service department was set up and what the advisors got used to doing in their first month.

Parts is quieter. A lot of what we find there came over in the conversion, because copying the old rules was the fastest way to hit the go-live date and everybody agreed they’d come back to it. Did anyone?

In sales and F&I the tell is paper. If a store is on a modern platform and the deal jacket is still a physical folder walking down the hall, something in the workflow didn’t get adopted, and it’s worth asking the F&I director which part and what stopped it. Accounting is usually the most disciplined office in the building and still the one with the most hand-built spreadsheets. Tekion’s terms give each implementation up to three hours of financial reporting training with a specialist. That’s a start. A controller in the middle of her first Tekion month-end is going to hold on to some of it and lose the rest, and that’s no knock on her.

What a Tekion Scorecard Finds

Everybody says optimization now. What does that actually mean? For us it means a scorecard. We measure a store’s results in Service, Parts, Sales and F&I, and Accounting against Tekion’s own benchmark data, so the dealer can see where the platform is already paying off and where there’s still value sitting there.

We’re running these scorecards right now, and we’re finding real lost opportunity in Tekion being misconfigured and in people not using things correctly. I’m not going to put a dollar figure in this post. We’ll have real data to share once clients have signed off on it, and until then a number from me is only a number.

What I can tell you is what the misses look like, because they repeat.

Settings that were copied from the old DMS to make the date. Nobody went back.
A feature that’s switched on and that three people use.
Releases. Something shipped last spring that would’ve helped the office, and nobody at the store heard about it, because the one person who read the release notes doesn’t work there anymore.
Roles and permissions that drifted as people changed jobs and kept their old access. That one’s a security problem as much as an efficiency problem, and we covered why in what dealers need to get right with Tekion.
New hires who learned Tekion from the person at the next desk, workarounds included.

None of those is dramatic. That’s a real problem, honestly, because a dramatic failure gets fixed by Friday and a quiet one runs for three years.

One Manager at a Time

A scorecard by itself is a report, and dealers have plenty of reports. Too many, probably. So we don’t drop it on the dealer principal’s desk and leave. We take it to the people who run the departments, one at a time. We’re going to talk to your service manager now. Here’s how your numbers sit against the benchmark, here’s what Tekion has brought out that your team isn’t using yet, and here’s how to use it. Then parts. Then the F&I director, and then the controller.

We basically hand-feed it to them, a piece at a time, because nobody running a service department has a free afternoon to go study a new workflow. Nobody does. That’s just the job they have.

A ski instructor told me something once, on terrain a good deal steeper than I wanted to be on. “You’re in it now. The only way out is through.” That’s a dealership after go-live. You can’t go back to the old system, and waiting until everybody feels ready isn’t a plan, so the only move left is to get good at the one you bought.

Helion Technologies is a Tekion Certified Partner. We’ve done dealership IT and nothing else for nearly 30 years, and today that’s 2,000-plus dealerships and 35,000 end users. We overlay all of that knowledge on what the benchmark shows, which is how we can tell a setting that’s wrong from one that’s just different. What we’re seeing lately, and this is my read and not anything Tekion has announced, is that as Tekion grows, more of this ongoing work gets done by certified partners working alongside Tekion’s own people. I think that’s healthy.

Who Owns Tekion After Go-Live?

Software needs feeding and care. Who owns that? I ask dealers all the time, and mostly what I get back is a pause.

The biggest mistake I ever made running Helion was promoting great technicians into leadership jobs. They were the best people we had at the technical work, so I figured they’d be the best at leading it, and I was wrong about that, because those turned out to be two different skills. I watch dealers make the same call with Tekion. They hand it to whoever’s best with computers. Wrong person. You want somebody who understands how the department makes its money, one name in each department and one name over all of them, and you want it to be part of the job they’re paid for. Give them the time. Put a review on the calendar every quarter and keep it the way you’d keep a meeting with your lender. And if you’re turning on AI agents that talk to your customers or touch your invoices, somebody has to set the rules for those too, which is why our managed AI for dealerships work exists in the first place.

If nobody inside has the hours, bring in help. I honestly don’t care whether that’s us or another certified partner, as long as somebody’s doing it. We built our DMS transition services around before, during, and after because of how often the after got skipped. Leaving it to feel is the mistake.
