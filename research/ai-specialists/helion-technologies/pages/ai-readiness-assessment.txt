AI is already in your stores. The question is what it can reach.

Cox Automotive’s AI in Auto Retail Tracker, published in August 2026, found that 82% of dealers already use AI, and only 22% of the dealers using it say it has grown sales or revenue so far. The risk lives there. A service advisor pastes repair order notes into a personal chat account, a BDC rep drafts lead replies in another one, and somebody in the office asks for administrator rights so they can try out a new AI tool. Nobody planned any of it. It just showed up.

The assessment is the first step of Managed AI for dealerships, the fifth pillar of what Helion Technologies does next to managed IT, cybersecurity, compliance and Tekion enablement, all run by one team. You request it through the complimentary IT and cybersecurity assessment, which already looks at AI tool usage. We’ve worked only with automotive and heavy truck dealerships since 1997, so we look at AI the way we look at everything else in a store, starting with what it touches and who can switch it off. Then we ask who answers for it.

There’s no pitch. We show you what AI could reach today, in writing, and then you decide.

FROM THE FIELD

What We Find When We Look at Dealership AI

Erik Nachbahr, Helion’s founder and a CISSP, put it plainly in an October 2026 webinar on AI governance for dealerships. “I have visibility into the stores that employ about 35,000 dealership employees,” he said, and what he sees across those stores is a readiness problem far more often than a tool problem. Those are his words, not ours.

The assessment is built to find the gaps on the right. Each quote comes from that talk, with the minute mark beside it so you can hear it in his own voice.

HEAR IT FROM ERIK

The readiness part of the talk starts at 14:27 in the AI governance webinar on YouTube. The full video and transcript are on our AI governance page.

Five gaps we keep finding
Erik Nachbahr, October 2026

14:55 A capable system on weak security

“You are bringing in a very capable system into a system that has limited security.” That’s the whole problem. AI doesn’t so much create new holes as find the ones that are already there, much faster than a person could, and it works with whatever access it’s been handed.

AI inherits the holes you already have.

15:04 Everyday users with admin rights

“We’re still seeing a lot of dealership employees that have administrative rights for everyday users.” It happens a lot. A new hire’s account gets copied from someone else’s and the permissions come along for the ride. Connect an AI tool through an account like that and it can read whatever the account can, including mailboxes and shared folders that belong to other people.

AI sees what the user sees.

15:27 No AI security training

“We’re not seeing dealers doing anything with AI security training.” Plenty of stores train people to spot phishing and spam. That’s a start. Very few have told the sales floor that pasting a customer’s credit application into a free chat account hands it to a company the dealership has no contract with.

Phishing training isn’t AI training.

15:43 Systems that aren’t ready for customer data

“We’re seeing systems that aren’t ready to host PII.” One dealership asked for a computer to run an in-house AI build and wanted it set up in an office closet. Erik called it “very 2008.” The customer information sitting in that box would be the same information the FTC Safeguards Rule already expects you to protect.

A box in a closet isn’t a plan.

40:17 No plan at all

“We’re seeing dealers going into AI without a plan at all.” Ask what the goal is and the honest answer is often that everyone else seems to be doing it. Then the tools pile up. Soon there’s a shared login or two, and nobody can say which one is holding customer data. Here’s what a dealership AI implementation plan should hold.

Start with the goal, then the tool.

THE CHECKLIST

What the AI Readiness Assessment Reviews

We review six areas. They tend to fail together. Few stores have everything in the right-hand column on day one. That’s normal. What matters is knowing which column you’re in before you connect anything new. For an outside yardstick, NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework starts in the same place, with governance and an inventory of the AI systems in use.

Area
	What we look at	Not ready yet	Ready
AI tools in use	Which tools each department runs, who signed up for them and whether the accounts are personal or owned by the dealership	Personal accounts on dealership workstations, or one login shared across the sales floor	Dealership-owned accounts only, from a list of approved tools
Customer data exposure	What customer and employee data people paste, upload or export, including DMS and CRM pulls saved to local PCs	Deal files and customer lists in free chat tools and in spreadsheets on desktops	Customer data stays in systems with a security contract and an audit trail
Microsoft 365 tenant	Sharing settings, guest access, multi-factor authentication and which AI features and connected apps are switched on	AI features and connected apps turned on with nobody checking what they can open	Each connected app approved on purpose, and every sign-in protected by multi-factor authentication
User rights	Who holds administrator rights and how new accounts get set up when someone is hired	Everyday users with admin rights, and new accounts copied from old ones	Each person reaches only what their job needs, reviewed on a schedule
Where data would live	Any plan to host an AI build, store exported data or connect AI to the DMS or CRM	A machine in a closet, or exported data that nobody owns	A private, controlled environment where AI can read the data but can’t take it anywhere
Policy, training and plan	A written AI use policy, staff sign-off, AI-specific security training and a stated goal	No policy, phishing training only and no goal beyond keeping up	A signed policy, AI security training and a goal leadership has agreed on

None of this is new law. The FTC Safeguards Rule already asks every dealership to limit each user’s access to the customer information they need and to give staff security awareness training that keeps up with new risks, and the FTC’s guide for businesses spells out both. AI raises the cost of skipping them. Our continuous compliance program keeps the records that show they’re in place.

What You Get at the End of the Assessment

The findings come to you in writing, addressed to the people who need to see them, so nothing gets lost in a hallway conversation. Nothing stays verbal. You’ll see which AI tools are in use, by whom and for what, which accounts and settings would let an AI tool reach further than it should, and what a governed program would look like next to the systems you already run.

Some of it is easy. A policy update or a cleanup of admin rights is often quick work for an internal IT person. The rest is a conversation about whether to bring in a governed AI workspace, and that conversation happens after you’ve read the report, never during the visit.

Read it first. Decide later.

A map of AI in use

Every AI tool we find, the department using it, whether the account belongs to the person or to the dealership, and what kind of data goes into it. Multi-store groups usually find more of it than leadership expected.

That part surprises people.

A written readiness report

The gaps in user rights, Microsoft 365 settings, data storage, training and policy, what each gap would let an AI tool reach, and what a governed setup alongside your current systems would look like.

It’s yours to keep.
