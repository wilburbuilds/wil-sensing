An Outage Hits the Sales Floor and the Service Drive First

Picture a Saturday with no files. The sales desk can’t pull a deal jacket, F&I can’t print a contract, and the service advisors are writing repair orders by hand while customers stand at the counter wondering why it’s taking so long. Nobody’s selling anything. When dealers get hit by a cyberattack, the downtime averages 3.4 weeks, according to CDK Global’s 2023 State of Cybersecurity in the Dealership study. Ransomware is only one way to get there. A failed server, a deleted mailbox or a bad sync can do it too. And when the DMS provider itself goes down, your own DMS outage plan is what keeps the doors open while they fix it.

Dealership backup and disaster recovery is the part of managed IT for dealerships that decides how fast you’re selling again. Helion Technologies runs it with the same team that handles your cybersecurity, so the people who spot an attack are the same people who bring the data back and get the desk working again. If you’re in the middle of an incident right now, our incident response plan template shows who does what in the first hours.

A backup you’ve never restored is a hope. We test ours before you need them.

SHARED RESPONSIBILITY

Microsoft puts this in writing. Its shared responsibility model leaves your data with you every time, whether it sits in Microsoft 365, in Azure or in your own building. Read across any row below. The middle column is the work that falls to the dealership, and it’s usually where nobody was assigned.

FROM THE FIELD

Where Erik Finds Dealers With No Backup at All

We asked Erik Nachbahr, Helion’s founder and a CISSP, where dealership backups fall apart. It isn’t the old server in the closet anymore. It’s the cloud, because moving there made people stop thinking about the copy. “They just don’t think about backups at all,” he says, and he sees it in three places.

Three gaps Erik sees in dealer clouds
Erik Nachbahr, Helion founder

Microsoft 365 Mail and files nobody backs up

“Microsoft doesn’t guarantee you backups of all of your data. It retains it for a bit, but that’s not a backup and restore, like what you typically expect.” A deleted email waits in Exchange Online for 14 days by default. A deleted OneDrive or SharePoint file waits 93. After that, it’s purged. The deal jacket that lived in a shared folder is gone for good. Our page on managed Microsoft 365 covers the rest of the tenant.

Retention isn’t a restore.

Azure servers The server moved. The backup didn’t.

A store lifts its accounting server or file server out of the closet and into Azure, which is often a smart move, and the backup box that used to sit next to the old server gets unplugged and forgotten. So whose job is the copy now? Microsoft answers that in its shared responsibility guide for reliability, and the answer is yours. Azure offers backups. Switching them on, and checking that they run, stays with the customer.

Offered isn’t switched on.

Private cloud Built in Azure with no snapshot plan

“Now you’re putting these mission-critical things in your own cloud, in private cloud, and Azure, and in 365, but you don’t actually have any backups of any of that information.” Larger groups build their own environment in Azure for the domain controllers, the file shares and the apps every store logs in to each morning. Most days it works fine. Then we ask when the last snapshot was taken and who checks that the job finished. Usually nobody knows.

Nobody owns it.

Disaster Recovery Only Counts if the Restore Works

Ransomware crews know where backups live. They check there first. CISA’s #StopRansomware Guide warns that many variants go looking for accessible backups and delete or encrypt them first, which is why it tells organizations to keep offline, encrypted copies and to test them regularly. A backup sitting on the same network, signed in with the same admin password, tends to go down with everything else. Catching the attacker before the encryption starts is the job of managed detection and response. The backup is the fallback.

The other half is time. A general manager doesn’t care that the data exists somewhere. The question is when the desk can work deals again and when the service drive can print a repair order, so we restore in that order and we time it during tests, not during the real thing. You’ll know the number. If your cyber insurance renewal asks how your backups are stored and when a restore was last tested, you’ll have the answer in writing. Our post on cyber recovery for dealerships walks through the steps after an attack.

Test it before you need it.

What CISA recommends

Offline, encrypted backups of critical data, delete protection or object lock on cloud storage, and regular tests that the backups are available and intact in a disaster recovery scenario.

Out of reach, and tested.

What a restore test proves

That the copy opens, that nothing important was left out of the job, and how many hours it takes to get the sales desk, the service drive and accounting working again.

A real recovery time, not a guess.
