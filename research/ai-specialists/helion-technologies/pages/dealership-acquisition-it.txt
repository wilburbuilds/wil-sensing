Dealership Acquisition IT Integration for Buy-Sells

When you buy a store, you buy its IT too. Helion finds what the seller let slide before closing, then moves the new rooftop onto your group’s standard.

You’re Buying the Store’s IT Problems Too

“When dealers are buying a dealership or a group of dealerships, they’re also buying their risks and problems from an IT standpoint.” That’s Erik Nachbahr, Helion’s founder, and he’s watched it happen for nearly 30 years. Everyone looks hard at the franchise, the real estate and the blue sky. The network cabinet in the parts hallway? Nobody opens it. Picture the store you just bought. The PCs behind the parts counter are years past replacement, the server in the back has no backup anyone has tested, and the seller’s IT vendor still has a remote login that nobody plans to switch off.

Dealership acquisition IT integration is the part of managed IT for dealerships that starts before the buy-sell closes. Helion Technologies looks at the target store while there’s still a price to negotiate, then moves it onto the same network, security and Microsoft 365 setup your other rooftops already run. The pre-close questions have their own list in our post on acquiring a dealership.

The seller won’t fix it for you. They’re selling in six months.

WHAT TRANSFERS AT CLOSING

What Comes With the Store in a Buy-Sell

Sellers stop spending on the way out. “They’re not gonna do that if they know that they’re gonna sell them in the next six months,” Erik says, and he’s right, because nobody upgrades a store they’re about to hand over. So you get whatever was there the day the spending stopped. Read across any row below. The middle column is what you usually find when nobody looked before closing.

What transfers
	What the buy-sell hands you	What it often looks like	What Helion does
The DMS	The seller’s DMS contract, its remaining term and every integration hanging off it	A platform your group doesn’t run, with years of deal and service history that has to come across	Maps the data and the integrations, then plans the conversion with your DMS provider and your controller
Network and circuits	Firewalls, switches, Wi-Fi and the internet circuits in every building	Gear past its support date, and carrier lead times that run longer than the closing schedule	Orders circuits early and builds the store’s network to the same design as your other rooftops
PCs, servers and licenses	Every workstation, server and software license in the store	Machines nobody replaced, and licenses nobody can prove were paid for	Counts and dates every device, checks proof of licensing and prices what has to be replaced
Email and Microsoft 365	The seller’s mailboxes, shared files and email domain	A separate Microsoft 365 tenant, and customers who keep writing to the old address	Moves mailboxes and files into your tenant, with a plan for mail still arriving at the old domain
Admin access	The global admin, firewall and domain registrar logins	Passwords held by the seller’s IT vendor or by an employee who isn’t coming over	Changes every admin login at closing and shuts off the old vendor’s remote access
Customer data	Deal jackets, credit applications and years of service history	Customer information your FTC Safeguards program now has to cover	Brings the store into your risk assessment, access controls and vendor oversight
Security and backups	Whatever endpoint protection, monitoring and backups the seller paid for	Subscriptions in the seller’s name that lapse when the store changes hands	Puts your monitoring and backups on the store’s systems as soon as it’s yours

Microsoft treats each tenant as the default boundary for collaboration and licensing, and its overview of multitenant organizations names mergers and acquisitions as the usual reason a company ends up with more than one. Its guide to cross-tenant mailbox migration requires a paid migration license for each user moved, with no exceptions. The customer data row comes from the Safeguards Rule itself, 16 CFR 314.4, which asks you to adjust your information security program for material changes to your operations or business arrangements. Our rundown of the FTC Safeguards Rule requirements covers the rest of the program.

FROM THE FIELD

Dealership Acquisition IT Integration, Phase by Phase

Erik has seen groups learn this the expensive way. They close first. IT comes second. “And then they buy a bunch of FTC risk, a bunch of liability, and huge costs,” he says. The fix isn’t a bigger IT budget after closing. It’s getting IT into the room while the deal is still being priced, and working in the order below.

ALREADY CLOSED?

It’s not too late. A complimentary IT and cybersecurity assessment of the new store shows what came with it and what to fix first.

Four phases of a buy-sell
The order Helion works in

Before you sign “What will this store cost us to fix?”

We look at the network, the PCs and servers, the licensing, Microsoft 365 and the security tools while there’s still a number to negotiate. Buy-sells are confidential. The people doing this work keep it that way. You get a written list of what’s out of date and what it’ll take to bring the store up to your standard, so the cost lands in the deal instead of in next year’s budget.

Price it before you own it.

Signing to close “Who needs to know, and when?”

Circuits get ordered. The DMS transition gets scheduled with your provider, and the Microsoft 365 move gets licensed and planned. Carriers and hardware suppliers don’t move at deal speed, and a new circuit into a building that never had a good one can take longer than the whole closing process, which is why most of the waiting happens here. We also write down every admin login the seller’s people hold.

Order early. Carriers are slow.

Closing day “Can the store open on Monday?”

Yes. Passwords change, the seller’s IT vendor loses its access and our monitoring goes onto the store’s machines. Anyone who isn’t coming over with the store loses their logins the same day. The sales floor keeps selling on what it ran on Friday until the cutover is ready.

Change the locks first.

After close “When will it look like our other stores?”

It depends on the store. The store moves onto your network, your Microsoft 365 tenant, your security and your DMS in an order that keeps the service drive writing repair orders. Then it joins your FTC Safeguards compliance program like every other rooftop, with its own place in the risk assessment. The new staff go through the same security awareness training as everyone else in the group.

One standard, every rooftop.

Four Acquisitions and Ten Relocations on One IT Standard

We see the same pattern every time a group grows. The new store arrives with its own setup, and the group’s IT person spends the next year running two of everything. Two DMS logins. Two email domains. One example from our case studies is a 23-store auto group in New England whose IT was getting more complicated than its internal team could keep up with. Helion supported that group through 4 dealership acquisitions and 10 store relocations without IT becoming the bottleneck, so each new store came onto the same standard as the stores the group already owned.

If your group has its own IT manager, they don’t get pushed aside. On the co-managed path they stay in charge, and we bring the extra hands an acquisition needs for a few busy months. Deals also draw attention from the wrong people, because a group in the middle of a closing is distracted, its staff are getting emails from people they’ve never met and passwords are changing hands every day. Our post on cybersecurity during dealership acquisitions covers that side.

Growth shouldn’t wait on IT.

What the group was doing

Adding 4 dealerships and moving 10 stores into new buildings, while the rest of its rooftops kept selling cars and writing repair orders.

IT stayed out of the way.

What a relocation adds

New circuits, new cabling and new Wi-Fi in a building nobody has worked in yet, planned before the moving trucks show up. Our post on IT in expansions and remodels has the details.
