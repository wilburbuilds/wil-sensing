Fast Isn’t a Number. 82 Seconds Is.

Every IT provider says its help desk is fast. Ask what fast means and you’ll usually get a feeling, not a figure. Ours is 82 seconds, the average time it takes one of your people to reach a live Helion technician, not a phone tree and not a callback queue. On a sales floor in the middle of a deal, a few minutes can decide whether you get a signed buyer’s order or a customer who’d like to think about it.

The help desk is one part of managed IT for dealerships, and this page covers that part alone. It covers who picks up, when they’re on, what they know about your DMS, and how the tickets split when you already have your own IT person on staff. It’s the piece of IT your staff actually touch.

Your people don’t judge IT by the firewall. They judge it by who answers the phone.

Built Around the Hours a Dealership Keeps

Dealerships don’t keep banker’s hours. The service drive is open before 7, the sales floor can still be writing deals at 9 at night, and Saturday is usually the busiest selling day of the week. NADA’s 2025 data counts more than 276 million repair orders written at franchised dealerships in a single year, and every one of them ran through a login, a printer and a network that somebody had to keep working. A help desk that shuts at 5 on Friday leaves your busiest hours on their own.

So our help desk is open whenever dealers are open, 7 days a week. If the service drive opens early or the sales floor is still writing deals late, a live technician picks up. After-hours emergencies and field support are covered at no extra charge, so a problem after closing or a technician in the building doesn’t turn into a second invoice. We’re open on July 4th and Labor Day on purpose, since those are big sale weekends, and we close only on Christmas Day and Thanksgiving Day. Out West? Same rule. Whenever your stores are open, the help desk is open too.

The Help Desk Is Also a Control Point

Erik Nachbahr, who founded Helion Technologies, told this one in a DealersEdge webinar in October 2026. A salesperson at a client store called our help desk and said, in Erik’s telling, “Hey, I need your help because I’m going to share my personal ChatGPT account with everybody else on the sales floor.” The caller wanted it installed on every computer and signed in as them on each one. As far as anyone could tell, nobody was being sneaky about it, and the salesperson had simply found a tool that made the job easier and wanted everybody on the floor to have it too.

Because it came in as a help desk call, the request landed in front of people who’ve watched this kind of thing go wrong before, rather than a well-meaning salesperson setting it up alone. That happens every day in smaller ways. A shared login here, a vendor asking for remote access there. The governed answer for AI is Managed AI, a private workspace the store owns, and Scot McConnor’s AI governance guide tells the whole story with the clip. You can also watch Erik tell it.

Sometimes the most useful thing a help desk does is ask a question first. On every call, the first one is who’s calling, and here’s why we start by verifying every caller’s identity.

Everyone reads every chat

If one login sits on every computer, every salesperson can scroll through everybody else’s chats, customer names and all.

Nothing stays private.

Customer data leaves the store

Whatever gets pasted in ends up in a personal account the dealership doesn’t own and has no way to look into.

It’s outside your controls.

Nobody can switch it off

The day that salesperson leaves, the account goes with them, along with any customer list they happened to upload.

And there’s no audit trail.
