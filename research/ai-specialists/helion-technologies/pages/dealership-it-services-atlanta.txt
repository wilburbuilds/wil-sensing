Three Rules an Atlanta Dealership Answers To

Has Georgia passed one of those California-style privacy laws yet? It hasn’t. Which leaves a single federal rule doing nearly all of the work, plus a state breach notice statute that reads narrower than most people in the business expect when they finally sit down with it. The table is short for a reason.

RULE	WHAT IT IS	DATE OR DEADLINE	AT THE STORE
FTC Safeguards Rule	
Federal rule for dealers that arrange financing or leasing
Applies	Amended rule in force June 9, 2023	A written security program, a qualified individual, multi-factor authentication, encryption, testing and vendor oversight. Get the checklist

No state law	Next attempt expected in 2027	Nothing new to comply with at the state level today. The federal rule sets the bar.
Georgia breach notification law	
O.C.G.A. Section 10-1-912
Ask your attorney	No fixed day count	Notice goes out in the most expedient time possible. Credit bureaus too, above 10,000 residents.

So where did the privacy bill go? It got as far as the Senate, which passed Senate Bill 111 in 2025 under the name Georgia Consumer Privacy Protection Act, and then the House threw out every line of it and wrote in something else, so the bill the governor signed on May 11, 2026 is about rural hospital tax credits. Same bill number. Nothing about privacy left in it.

None of that shrinks the federal job. The Safeguards Rule in 16 CFR 314.4 still expects a written security program, plus a notice to the FTC within 30 days when a breach touches 500 or more consumers. The state statute is written for information brokers and government agencies, though the attorney general’s consumer office describes it as covering businesses that hold unencrypted personal records, which is why we say ask your attorney. Our dealership compliance team builds the program either way.

Dealership Cybersecurity in Atlanta, GA

June 2024 is the month every dealer remembers. Asbury Automotive Group, the public dealer group headquartered in Duluth, told investors in an SEC filing that the CDK Global attack hit its sales, service, inventory, customer relationship management and accounting functions. Stores kept selling and servicing cars. Slowly. The company later estimated the outage cost it between $0.95 and $1.15 per diluted share that quarter. We said it then and still mean it. CDK wasn’t a one-time wake-up call.

Nobody at a store can stop a vendor outage. What a store can control is its own side of the wire, and that’s where our dealership cybersecurity work lives. A live, US-based security operations center watches the network day and night. Multi-factor authentication goes on email and remote access. Backups get a test restore, because a backup nobody has ever restored is only a hope. The wider picture is in the state of dealership cybersecurity.
