Dealership IT Services in Boston Answer to a 2010 Rule

Ask a Massachusetts dealer when they wrote their first information security program and the honest answer is often “when the FTC made us, in 2023.” The state got there first. Since March 1, 2010, anyone holding personal information about a Massachusetts resident has had to keep a written security program, and a dealership holds plenty of it in every deal jacket, credit application and service record. Nearly every store qualifies.

That’s the first thing that sets this market apart. The second is how often a group crosses a state line. A store in Burlington, another in Nashua and a third in Warwick is a normal footprint here, and each of those states writes its own rules. Each one counts.

We’ve worked through rules like these since 1997. Helion Technologies supports automotive and heavy truck dealerships and nobody else, and this page is part of our dealership IT services by location hub. Here’s what’s different about Boston and the states around it.

What the Massachusetts Security Rule Asks of a Dealership

The regulation is 201 CMR 17.00, and it applies to any business that owns or licenses personal information about a Massachusetts resident, wherever the business sits. Somebody at the store has to own the program. Staff and contractors get trained on it, and someone looks it over at least once a year, or sooner when the business changes in a way that touches customer data. Vendors count too. They have to be able to protect what you hand them, and your contract has to say they will.

The technical list is where stores fall short. Personal information on laptops and other portable devices has to be encrypted, and so does anything sent over a public or wireless network. Each person needs their own login, not a shared one taped under the keyboard at the parts counter. Accounts lock after too many bad passwords, and a salesperson who quits on Friday loses access on Friday, not the week after. Add current patches, a firewall, anti-malware and monitoring for anyone who shouldn’t be in the system. That’s a lot of IT. Few stores check every box.

Here’s the good news. The FTC’s amended Safeguards Rule asks for much of the same, so one well-built program can answer both. We keep the technical side running and documented, and our dealership compliance team keeps the program current. If you’re starting from zero, read what a comprehensive information security program is first, then the FTC Safeguards Rule requirements.

Six lines matter for a group with stores around Boston. One federal rule, two Massachusetts rules, and three newer state privacy acts that treat dealers very differently from one state to the next.

Massachusetts still has no comprehensive consumer privacy law. The House and Senate each passed a version in 2026, and as this page is written the two are still being worked out in a conference committee. Nothing is signed yet. Until it is, the breach law and the security regulation carry the load. The breach law wants notice as soon as practicable and without unreasonable delay, and it says plainly that not knowing the final count yet is no reason to hold it back.

The neighbors keep firmer clocks. Rhode Island gives a business 45 days from confirming a breach, and brings in its Attorney General and the credit bureaus above 500 residents. Connecticut allows 60 days from discovery, with the Attorney General told no later than the customers. New Hampshire says as soon as possible. Plan for the shortest clock. Then there’s the FTC, whose rule in 16 CFR 314.4 adds its own 30-day notice once 500 or more consumers are involved. Your attorney decides how each rule applies to you, and our compliance team keeps the program that answers all of them. Stores further afield? Compare them in dealership privacy laws by state, and follow rule changes in FTC Safeguards Rule news.

Dealership Cybersecurity in Boston

Picture the morning after a February nor’easter. The showroom’s closed. Nobody’s at the store. The plows are still out, and the business office is finishing deals from kitchen tables on company laptops. If those laptops aren’t encrypted and the remote connection only asks for a password, that’s the day an attacker has the easiest time of the year. It’s also exactly what the Massachusetts rule was written to prevent.

CDK Global’s 2024 survey of dealers found that 35% had a cyberattack or incident that year, and 92% of them said it did real financial or operational damage. June 2024 then showed what a vendor outage does to a store that never planned for one. Our dealership cybersecurity work covers the store’s side. A live, US-based security operations center watches the network through managed detection and response, advanced endpoint detection and response guards the computers, and multi-factor authentication goes on email and remote access. Microsoft 365 security closes the inbox where most attacks start, and security awareness training teaches everyone with a login to spot the fakes. Penetration testing and vulnerability scanning shows where an attacker would get in, and our look at auto dealership ransomware shows how those attacks usually begin. If one gets through anyway, incident response for dealerships takes it from there.
