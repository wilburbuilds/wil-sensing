Dealership IT Services in Chicago Start With the Time Clock

Walk into almost any service department in the suburbs and ask how the techs clock in. A lot of the time the answer is a finger on a scanner by the door. In most states that’s a payroll detail. In Illinois it’s a legal question, because the state has a biometric privacy law with its own consent rules and its own right to sue, and a dealership’s IT is usually what collects and stores those scans.

That’s one thing that makes this market different. The other is the state line. Plenty of groups with stores in Schaumburg or Orland Park also run a rooftop in Merrillville or Schererville, and Indiana has its own breach law with a hard deadline.

The Biometric Question Illinois Dealers Ask First

The Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act covers fingerprints, hand scans and face geometry. Before a business collects any of them, it needs a written policy that’s available to the public, with a retention schedule, and it needs informed written consent from each person. The Act also lets individuals sue. A 2024 amendment limited that to one recovery per person rather than one per scan, which helped, but the consent and retention rules didn’t change at all.

There’s a wrinkle that matters to dealers. Section 25(c) says the Act doesn’t apply to a financial institution subject to the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, and a store that arranges financing usually is one. We haven’t found a court ruling on a dealership, though, so treat that as an argument your attorney can make, not something to build a time clock policy on. The Illinois entry in our state privacy law guide has the same caution.

The IT work is the part we handle. We find every device that takes a scan (time clocks, door readers, the key cabinet), map where the templates are stored and which vendor holds them, and set the deletion schedule your policy promises. Your attorney writes the policy. Our dealership compliance team makes sure the systems do what it says.

Illinois and Indiana Rules for Chicago Dealerships

Five lines matter here. One federal rule does most of the work, Illinois adds a breach law and the biometric law, and Indiana adds a breach law with a firm deadline plus a newer privacy act that most dealers won’t fall under.

Illinois still has no comprehensive consumer privacy law of the Virginia kind, so the breach law is where most of the work sits. The Personal Information Protection Act wants customers told in the most expedient time possible and without unreasonable delay, and once more than 500 Illinois residents are involved, the Attorney General has to hear about it no later than they do. Indiana’s breach law is stricter on time. It puts a 45-day outer limit on the notice and copies the state Attorney General every time.

A group with stores in Naperville and Merrillville could owe both on the same morning, plus the FTC. The federal rule in 16 CFR 314.4 needs a written security program and its own 30-day notice to the FTC once 500 or more consumers are involved. Your attorney decides how each rule applies to you, and our compliance team keeps the program that answers all of them. Stores in other states too? Dealership Cybersecurity in Chicago

Pull a list of every login at a busy Chicago store and read it with the controller. You’ll almost always find a salesperson who left in the spring, a former F&I manager whose email still forwards somewhere and a vendor account nobody remembers creating. Each of those is a door. Attackers don’t need to break a window when a key is still hanging by the back entrance.

CDK Global’s 2024 survey of dealers found that 35% had a cyberattack or incident that year, and 92% of them said it did real financial or operational damage.
