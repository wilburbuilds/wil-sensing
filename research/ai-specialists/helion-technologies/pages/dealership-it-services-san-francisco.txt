Dealership IT Services in San Francisco Start With the Map

Ask a Bay Area dealer how far apart their stores are and you’ll get the answer in bridges, not miles. A group with one store on Serramonte Boulevard in Colma, another in Fremont and a third off the Capitol Expressway in San Jose has the whole bay sitting in the middle of its network. It’s still one DMS, one phone system and one set of logins, and every rooftop is expected to behave as if it shared a wall with the others. That’s harder than it sounds.

The other thing that sets this market apart is the ground under it. Earthquakes, fire-season power shutoffs and the occasional cut fiber line all land here, and a store that never planned for them learns the hard way what a day without the DMS costs.

We’ve been planning for days like that since 1997. Helion Technologies supports automotive and heavy truck dealerships and nobody else, and this page is part of our dealership IT services by location hub. Here’s what’s specific to San Francisco and the counties around the bay.

A US-based team monitors networks, servers and security alerts 24/7, straight through the night Pacific time.

When the Bay Area Shakes or the Power Goes Off

The US Geological Survey puts the odds of at least one magnitude 6.7 or larger earthquake striking the San Francisco Bay region before 2043 at 72%. We don’t read that as a scare line. It’s a planning number, and anyone who was here for Loma Prieta in October 1989 remembers how one quake can take the power, the phones and the roads all at the same time. Nobody forgets that. The Hayward fault runs right under the East Bay, through the same cities where plenty of stores line I-880.

Fire season brings a quieter version of the same problem. On dry, windy days PG&E can switch off power to whole neighborhoods so its lines don’t start a wildfire, and those Public Safety Power Shutoffs can last for days, sometimes with only a few hours of warning. A service drive can’t write a repair order on a dead network. The phones go quiet too.

None of this calls for heroics. It calls for a plan that’s written down before the day comes. We keep copies of your data outside the Bay Area and prove they actually restore, put battery backup on the network and phone gear, and agree with you ahead of time which systems come back first. That’s the work behind backup and disaster recovery for dealerships, and our DMS outage plan covers the day the problem belongs to your vendor instead of you.

An outage that exposes customer data is also a legal event in California, and this state doesn’t give dealers the pass most others do. The California Consumer Privacy Act exempts only the information collected under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, not the dealership itself, so service records, website leads and marketing lists stay covered. That’s the catch. Since January 1, 2026, the state’s breach law, as amended by Senate Bill 446, gives a business 30 calendar days from discovery to notify the people affected, and the FTC’s rule in 16 CFR 314.4 adds its own 30-day notice once 500 or more consumers are involved. Thirty days goes quickly when nobody kept the logs.

We’ve worked through California’s rules with dealers since 2019, when Helion partnered with a statewide association of franchised new car dealers to get its members ready for the CCPA. That story, along with the three rules every California store answers to, is on our Los Angeles page. Your attorney decides how each rule applies to you, and our dealership compliance team keeps one written program that answers to all of them. Start with a Safeguards risk assessment template. Dealership Cybersecurity in San Francisco

A lot of the AI tools your staff already use were built within a few miles of your stores, and they’re showing up in the business office. A BDC rep pastes a customer’s lead into a free chatbot to draft a reply, or a service advisor asks one to summarize a repair history before a callback. It saves time. Nobody means any harm by it. But that customer’s information just left the store on a personal account with no audit trail, and in a state where customers can sue after a breach, that’s worth fixing before it becomes a story.

CDK Global’s 2024 survey of dealers found that 35% had a cyberattack or incident that year, and 92% of them said it did real financial or operational damage. Our dealership cybersecurity work covers the store’s side. A live, US-based security operations center watches the network through managed detection and response, advanced endpoint detection and response guards the computers, and multi-factor authentication goes on email and remote access. Microsoft 365 security closes the inbox where most attacks start, security awareness training teaches everyone with a login to spot the fakes, and penetration testing shows where an attacker would get in.

For the AI side, Managed AI gives staff a private, governed workspace in place of personal accounts, and our piece on public AI and customer data explains what’s at stake. Not sure whether you need an IT provider or a security one? Read MSP vs MSSP for dealerships. Want to know where your logins, backups and AI use stand today? Where We Work Around the Bay Area

California has 1,334 franchised new-car dealers, according to NADA Data 2025, more than any other state, and the Bay Area’s share of them is spread all the way around the water, from the auto rows on the Peninsula to the malls in Fremont and along San Jose’s Capitol Expressway, and out through the Tri-Valley and Contra Costa. It’s a big market. Family groups grew large here, like Putnam, which opened its first Buick store in Burlingame in 1965. Truck dealers work the same freeways that feed the Port of Oakland, and Coast Counties Peterbilt runs branches from San Jose to San Leandro. We mention both for scale, not as clients. Franchised dealers statewide are represented by the California New Car Dealers Association.

We know what fast growth does to a California group’s IT. One Southern California auto group we support had added stores one at a time with no single IT plan behind them, and by overhauling its infrastructure with us it saved more than $400,000.
