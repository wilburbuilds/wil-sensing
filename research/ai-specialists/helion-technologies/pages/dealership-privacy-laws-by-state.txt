Dealership Privacy Laws by State: What Applies to Your Rooftops

About two dozen states have passed a consumer privacy law, and most of them exempt a dealer that arranges financing. A few don’t. Here’s the state-by-state read for the places you sell.

Most dealership privacy laws by state exempt a dealer that arranges financing, a few such as California’s exempt only the financing data, and about half the states have no consumer privacy law, only breach notice and security rules.

Fifty states, three patterns. Know which one each rooftop sits in.

A store that arranges financing or leases is a financial institution under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, which is why every dealer already answers to the FTC Safeguards Rule requirements. That federal rule is the floor in all fifty states. State law sits on top of it, and the states haven’t agreed on much, which is how a dealer group with rooftops in three states ends up reading three different statutes to answer what sounds like one simple question about customer data. Some wrote a privacy law and waved dealers through. A few didn’t wave. Automakers add their own cybersecurity requirements on top of both.

Helion Technologies has worked only with automotive and heavy truck dealerships since 1997, and state law comes up in nearly every dealership cybersecurity conversation we have with a group that crosses a state line. The question is always the same one, asked by a controller or a general manager who’s just heard about a new law from a vendor with something to sell, and who wants to know whether it’s real before spending money on it. Usually it’s half real.

We’ve written about several of these laws one at a time, and those posts are linked from each state below. This page is the map. Find your state, read the entry, then follow the link for the detail.

The GLBA Exemption Decides Most of It

State data privacy laws for car dealerships turn on one clause. Nearly every state privacy law carves out the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act somehow, because lawmakers didn’t want two sets of rules for the same loan file. How they wrote that carve-out is what matters to you. There are two versions, and they lead to very different amounts of work.

Read the clause for each state where you have a store or a meaningful number of customers. Then read it again next year. Legislatures have started trimming the broad version, and a dealership that was exempt as a whole business in January can find itself covered for its service and marketing data by the following January without a single thing changing inside the store. Our FTC Safeguards compliance work covers the federal half, and the same written program does most of the lifting for the state half.

Same store. Different answer.

Entity-level exemption

The law doesn’t apply to a financial institution subject to the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act. A dealership that arranges financing is one, so the whole business sits outside the privacy law. Texas, Virginia, Maryland, Colorado, New Jersey and Florida are written this way.

The store is out. The breach law still applies.

Data-level exemption

Only the information handled under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act is exempt. The credit application is out. The service customer who paid cash, the website visitor, the lead who never applied and your own employees are all still in. California is written this way, and Oregon, Minnesota, Montana and Connecticut now treat a dealership the same.

The loan file is out. Everything else is in.

States with a consumer privacy law
Checked October 2026

CA California

California is the one that bites. The California Consumer Privacy Act exempts information handled under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act and nothing else, so a store over the thresholds (revenue above $26,625,000, or data on 100,000 consumers or households) owes privacy rights to service customers, web visitors, leads and employees. The agency has already fined two automakers over website opt-outs. Since January 2026 a breach notice is due within 30 days. Our post on the California Privacy Rights Act explains how the agency came to be, and our Los Angeles dealership IT page covers the local side. For the Bay Area, our San Francisco dealership IT page adds an outage plan for earthquakes and power shutoffs.

Only the financing data is exempt.

CO Colorado

The Colorado Privacy Act has been in force since July 2023, and it exempts a financial institution subject to the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act along with its affiliates. Your store is out. What still applies is the older security law, which requires reasonable security, a written disposal policy and breach notice within 30 days. That older law is the subject of our Colorado blueprint for auto and truck dealers. Our Denver dealership IT page lines those rules up next to Colorado’s new automated decision law, SB 26-189.

Dealership exempt. The security law still applies.

DE Delaware

Watch this one. Delaware’s law exempts financial institutions today, but House Bill 380, signed in September 2026, rewrites that exemption from January 1, 2027 so it covers banks, credit unions, insurers and securities firms and leaves everyone else with only the data carve-out. A dealership isn’t on the list. The same bill drops the threshold from 35,000 consumers to 10,000, which a busy store can reach on service traffic alone. Both versions sit side by side in the Delaware Code.

Exempt until 2027. Then only the financing data.

FL Florida

Florida’s Digital Bill of Rights sounds broad and isn’t. It applies to companies with more than $1 billion in global revenue that also run an app store, a smart speaker or an ad business, and it exempts Gramm-Leach-Bliley financial institutions on top of that. No dealership fits. The law that matters here is the Florida Information Protection Act, which gives you 30 days to notify customers after a breach and requires notice to the Department of Legal Affairs when 500 or more Floridians are affected. See our Miami dealership IT and Tampa Bay dealership IT pages.

Not aimed at dealers. Mind the 30-day breach clock.

MD Maryland

Maryland’s law took effect October 1, 2025 and is one of the strictest on what a business may collect. It exempts a financial institution, its affiliates and Gramm-Leach-Bliley data, so a store that arranges financing sits outside it. The breach law doesn’t let you off. Notice is due within 45 days, and the attorney general must be told before your customers are. We explain the law in why Maryland’s privacy law matters to dealers everywhere, and our Baltimore dealership IT page has the local rules.

Dealership exempt. Attorney general notified first.

NJ New Jersey

In force since January 15, 2025. The New Jersey Data Privacy Act doesn’t apply to a financial institution, its affiliates or data subject to the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, which takes a dealership out. The breach statute is unusual. It sets no number of days, and it sends your first call to the Division of State Police, before any customer hears from you. North Jersey stores are covered on our New York metro dealership IT page.

Dealership exempt. State Police before customers.

TX Texas

The Texas Data Privacy and Security Act has no revenue or customer-count threshold, which makes it look like it covers everybody. It doesn’t cover you. Section 541.002 says the chapter doesn’t apply to a financial institution subject to the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act. Texas enforces its breach law hard, though. Customers get notice within 60 days and the attorney general within 30 when 250 or more Texans are involved. Our Houston and Dallas-Fort Worth pages cover the local picture, and our Texas Data Privacy and Security Act guide for dealers covers where the exemption stops.

Dealership exempt. Two breach clocks.

VA Virginia

Virginia wrote the template most states copied. Section 59.1-576 says the law doesn’t apply to a financial institution or data subject to the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, and that sentence shows up nearly word for word in Texas and Florida. Breach notice goes out without unreasonable delay. No day count.

Dealership exempt. The original version of the clause.

SEVEN WITHOUT ONE

No State Privacy Law Doesn’t Mean No Rules

These states and the District haven’t passed a consumer privacy law. Every one of them has a breach notice statute, and several add security, disposal or biometric rules that reach a dealership directly.

The federal clock runs alongside all of them. Our guide to the Safeguards Rule notification requirement covers the 30-day notice to the FTC. North and South Carolina haven’t passed one either, and our Charlotte dealership IT page compares their two breach laws side by side.

ONE PLAN, EVERY CLOCK

A group with stores in three states can face four notice deadlines after one incident. Put each one in the incident response plan before you need it.

States without a consumer privacy law
Checked October 2026

DC District of Columbia

The District has no consumer privacy law, and its breach law has one of the lowest reporting triggers in the country. Fifty residents. At that point the attorney general gets written notice, and a breach that exposes Social Security numbers means 18 months of identity theft protection at your cost. A business that meets the federal Safeguards Rule is treated as meeting the District’s security requirement. Our Washington, DC dealership IT page covers Maryland and Virginia alongside it.

Fifty residents triggers attorney general notice.

GA Georgia

Georgia came close. Senate Bill 111 passed the Senate as a consumer privacy act in 2025, then the House swapped out its text, and it was signed in May 2026 as a rural hospital tax credit bill with no privacy language left in it. So there’s nothing new at the state level. The breach statute is written for information brokers and government data collectors, which is why we tell Georgia dealers to ask their attorney how it applies to them instead of assuming either way. More on our Atlanta dealership IT page.

No state law. The federal rule sets the bar.

IL Illinois

No consumer privacy law here. Two bills stalled in committee in 2026. Illinois dealers ask us about the Biometric Information Privacy Act instead, because fingerprint time clocks are common in service departments and the Act lets individuals sue for $1,000 to $5,000 a violation. Section 25(c) says the Act doesn’t apply to a financial institution subject to the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act. Courts have applied that to universities that make student loans. We haven’t found a ruling on a dealership, so treat it as an argument your attorney can make, not a guarantee. Our Chicago dealership IT page covers the devices that take the scans and the Indiana breach law next door.

Fingerprint time clock? Ask your attorney first.

MA Massachusetts

Massachusetts may be next. The Senate passed a data privacy act 40 to 0 in 2025, the House passed its own version 146 to 0 in June 2026, and a conference committee is working out the differences. It isn’t law yet. What already applies is 201 CMR 17, which requires a written information security program from any business that holds personal information on a Massachusetts resident, wherever that business is located. Every breach goes to the attorney general and the consumer affairs office. Our Boston dealership IT page lays out the rules in Massachusetts and its three neighbors side by side.

A written security program is already required.

NY New York

The New York Privacy Act has been introduced year after year and hasn’t passed either house. The SHIELD Act did, back in 2019, and it has teeth. It requires reasonable safeguards, although a business that complies with the Gramm-Leach-Bliley rules is treated as compliant with that part. The breach side got stricter in December 2024, when the state added a hard 30-day deadline. Not sure where you stand? We wrote a SHIELD Act readiness check for dealers. Start with what dealerships need to know about the SHIELD Act. Our New York dealership IT page lines up the New York, New Jersey and Connecticut breach deadlines side by side.

Thirty days. No exceptions for size.

OH Ohio

Ohio took a different road. Its Data Protection Act doesn’t require anything. It rewards you. A business with a written cybersecurity program that conforms to a recognized framework gets an affirmative defense if it’s sued over a breach, and the statute lists the Gramm-Leach-Bliley security rules as one of those frameworks. A real Safeguards program does double duty in Ohio. Breach notice is due within 45 days. See our guide to the Ohio Data Protection Act.

Your Safeguards program can be a legal defense.

PA Pennsylvania

Still pending. House Bill 78 passed the House 127 to 76 in October 2025 and has been moving through the Senate since, with its last recorded action on October 6, 2026. The Senate’s version exempts financial institutions and Gramm-Leach-Bliley data, so a dealership would likely sit outside it if it passes as written. The breach law changed in 2024. The attorney general now gets notice whenever more than 500 Pennsylvanians do. Our Philadelphia dealership IT page follows the bill.

Not law yet. Check again after the session.

How to Work Out Which State Privacy Laws Apply to Your Rooftops
1
List every state you touch

Start with where the stores are. Then add the states your customers live in, because most of these laws follow the resident, not the rooftop.

2
Read the exemption

For each state, find out whether the law exempts the dealership or only the financing data. It’s one clause. Ask your attorney to confirm it.

3
Sort your data

Where only the data is exempt, split what you hold. Credit applications go on one side, and service records, web leads and employee files go on the other.

4
Write down every clock

Put each state’s breach deadline in the incident response plan beside the FTC’s 30 days. Then build security to the strictest rule you found.
