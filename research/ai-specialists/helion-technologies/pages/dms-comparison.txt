DMS Comparison: CDK vs Reynolds vs Tekion vs Dealertrack

An IT provider’s view of four dealer management systems, and the network, phone and data work each one hands to the people who keep your stores running.

This DMS comparison for car dealerships looks at CDK, Reynolds, Tekion and Dealertrack from the IT side. All four run the core of a franchised store. The differences that land on IT are hosting, third-party data access, integrations and what else each vendor sells you.

The logo on the DMS changes. The network under it doesn’t.

Erik Nachbahr, who founded Helion Technologies in 1997, sums up what most DMS comparisons leave out with one line about the invoice. “CDK will provide phone systems, they’ll provide network.” Plenty of stores have paid for all of it on one bill for so long that nobody remembers those were ever separate purchases. That’s the bundle. A DMS decision is the moment to look at each of those lines on its own. Moving from CDK to Tekion? Here’show to rethink the phones and network in that move.

Search for a DMS comparison and you’ll mostly find feature grids and software review sites. Very little of it covers the circuits, the phones, the data connections and the user accounts that every one of these systems depends on, so this page does, for dealers weighing our DMS transition support or our Tekion enablement services. The demo won’t tell you which of the 10+ vendors in a typical store loses its data feed on cutover weekend. We will.

Full disclosure. Helion is a Tekion Certified Partner, and we also support stores running CDK, Reynolds and Dealertrack. So we don’t rank them. Everything below is what each company publishes about itself.

Data Access Is the DMS Question That Grew Up With AI

Every AI tool a dealer gets pitched this year, from a service scheduling assistant to a desking add-on to a voice agent that books appointments, has to read DMS data and sometimes write it back. How that connection gets approved, what it costs and how it gets shut off is now an IT question as much as a software one. It’s both now.

Erik’s view, after decades of wiring vendors into dealer systems, comes down to control. Is there a real, customer-facing API? Does the dealer decide which vendors get in? Those two answers tell you most of what you need.

All four companies publish a program for third-party access, and the programs don’t work the same way. Ask early. Read each one’s terms before the AI pitch, not after, and keep a written list, updated whenever a vendor is added or dropped, of every vendor that holds a connection today, because most stores can’t produce that list on the first ask. If AI is why you’re asking, our managed AI page covers the governed side.

Six IT Questions to Ask Every DMS Vendor

Feature demos are built to impress. These questions are built to be answered in writing, by all four vendors, in the same format, so you can lay the answers next to each other and see the IT work behind each proposal. Ask all six.

Dealership DMS vendor review
Same six questions for all four

1 What else is on the DMS invoice?

Phones, circuits, firewalls, Wi-Fi and help desk services can all ride on a DMS bill, and those contracts usually carry their own terms and end dates. Check every one. Whichever DMS you choose, a DMS decision is the natural time to re-bid each of them on its own merits.

Highlight every line that isn’t the DMS itself.

2 How does a new vendor get access to our data?

Ask how a connection is requested and approved, who pays for it, how fast it can be switched off and what a vendor is allowed to write back into your deal, customer and repair order records. Then ask for the list of every third party connected to your store today. Lists go stale fast.

Keep the connection list in your vendor file.

3 Where does it run, and what happens when the circuit drops?

A hosted DMS makes the internet circuit part of the system. No circuit, no DMS. Plan a second circuit from a different carrier with automatic failover, and ask each vendor what it needs from your network. Our Tekion network requirements guide shows what that list looks like for one platform.

Unplug the main circuit on a Tuesday and watch.

4 Which of our integrations are already certified?

F&I menus, credit bureaus, OEM parts ordering, payroll, the CRM and the phone system all connect to the DMS. Ask each of those vendors whether it’s in the new platform’s program today, not on a roadmap. Roadmaps slip.

Get the certification in writing.

5 Which devices have to change?

Impact printers in accounting, laser printers in the F&I office, signature pads, check scanners and label printers in parts each need a driver, a setting or a replacement. Accounting’s printer counts too. Old PCs slow down any system, so check their age before go-live.

Test every printer with a real document.

6 How do user access and the Safeguards file change?

Build roles from today’s org chart, turn on multi-factor authentication for every user and remove accounts for people who’ve left. Old logins linger. The FTC Safeguards Rule also expects you to oversee the DMS vendor as a service provider.

Ask how your data comes out if you ever leave.

The same work at every store, sequenced in waves, with each wave learning from the last.

There’s no wrong answer on this page. Size changes the math. A single-point store with one office manager who also runs payroll weighs these very differently from a dealer group with its own IT director (the case for co-managed IT), four OEMs and two DMS platforms already running side by side after an acquisition.
