A template gets the plan on paper. A drill makes it work.

Picture the service drive at 7:15 on a Monday morning. The DMS won’t open. The phones are ringing, and the controller wants to know whether anybody has called the insurance company yet, which nobody has, because nobody is sure whose job that is. A 2023 dealership cybersecurity study from CDK Global put the average downtime after an attack at 3.4 weeks. That’s a long time. The first few hours are where a plan earns its keep.

This template gives those hours a script. It follows the seven areas the FTC Safeguards Rule lists for a written incident response plan, in the order you’d reach for them, with blanks for the names, numbers and decisions that belong to your store. It’s free. It’s yours to change. It isn’t legal advice, either. The plan sits inside your wider dealership cybersecurity program, and it only works if the people named in it have practiced it at least once.

One rule before you start. If a name in the plan has left the building, the plan has a hole in it.

Which Dealerships Need a Written Incident Response Plan

Any dealership that arranges financing or leasing counts as a financial institution under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, so the FTC Safeguards Rule applies to it. Cars or heavy trucks, one rooftop or twenty. The test is the same. The written incident response plan is one of the rule’s nine required elements, and paragraph (h) of section 314.4 spells out what it has to cover.

Size changes one thing. Under section 314.6 of the rule, a dealership that keeps customer information on fewer than 5,000 consumers doesn’t have to put the plan in writing. That’s the only break. It still has to deal with an incident when one happens, though, and the FTC breach notice applies at every size, so a short written version is worth the hour it takes.

Small store or not, write it down anyway.

Fewer than 5,000 consumers

The written plan is optional for you, along with the written risk assessment, the fixed testing schedule and the annual report to your board. The 30-day FTC notice for a breach involving 500 or more consumers still applies.

A one-page version of this template still pays off.

5,000 consumers or more

The plan has to exist in writing and address all seven areas. A store that has financed cars for a few years usually crosses this line once the old deal files are counted.

Fill in every section.

IN THE RULE

What the FTC Safeguards Rule Requires in an Incident Response Plan

Section 314.4(h) names seven areas. The wording is yours. Here’s each one, what it looks like at a dealership, and where it sits in the template below.

Area the rule names
	What it means at your store	Where it is in the template

Internal response processes	The steps from the first report to the all-clear. Who gets called, what gets unplugged, what gets saved.	Sections 4 and 5
Roles, responsibilities and decision authority	Who runs the response, who can take the network offline and who can approve spending on outside help.	Section 2
External and internal communications	What staff, customers, lenders, your OEM, your insurer and regulators hear, and who tells them.	Sections 3, 6 and 7
Remediation of weaknesses	Closing the hole the attacker used, not just restoring from backup and hoping.	Section 8

THE TEMPLATE

The Dealership Incident Response Plan Template

Ten sections, in the order you’d reach for them during a bad morning. Each one says what belongs there and leaves blanks for your store’s answers. Use pencil first. Fill it in at one sitting with your general manager, your controller and whoever runs IT, since they’re the people who’ll be named in it. Bring coffee.

1 Goals and scope

Say what this plan protects and what sets it off. Keep it short. Customer information in the DMS, the CRM, email and the F&I office is in scope, and so are paper deal jackets. Under the rule, a security event is anything that gives someone unauthorized access to those systems or that information, or disrupts or misuses them. A lost laptop counts. So does a ransom note on the parts counter printer.

Covers 314.4(h)(1).

2 Response team and who decides

Somebody has to be in charge, and at most stores that’s the Qualified Individual, with a backup who can step in when the lead is on a plane. Write in who’s allowed to make the expensive calls too, like taking every store offline, hiring a forensics firm or answering a ransom demand, because those decisions tend to sit until the one person who can say yes picks up the phone. One dealer group we support lost its whole internal IT team, CIO included, overnight when it split from eight stores into three, which is exactly why every role here gets a second name. Our advice on ransomware payments hasn’t changed in years. Don’t pay.

3 Contact sheet, printed

Paper matters more here than anywhere else in the plan. When ransomware locks the file server, the contact list saved on it goes with it, so print the numbers and keep one copy in the general manager’s desk and one at the incident lead’s house. Your DMS provider’s support line belongs on the list as well, because vendors get breached too, and truck dealers found that out in 2025 when the Motility DMS breach reached their customers. Check the list twice a year. The departments need their own version too: our DMS downtime procedures guide covers how sales, service, parts, and payroll keep running on paper.

4 The first hour

The first person to notice may not work in IT at all. It could be a BDC rep whose screen fills with a ransom note, or a title clerk who suddenly can’t get into the DMS, so the opening line of this section is simply who they call. After that, the machine comes off the network but stays powered on, since shutting it down can erase what an investigator needs to see how the attacker got in. Nobody wipes, reinstalls or pays anything until your security team says so. If your IT lead has ten minutes this week, CISA’s StopRansomware guide covers the same ground in more depth.

Covers 314.4(h)(2).

5 Investigate and size it

Once it’s contained, somebody sizes it up, and that means the stores and systems that were hit, the customer records involved, and whether anything was copied out or only locked where it sits. If the trouble started in email, a Microsoft 365 breach assessment shows whether someone is still inside. Write down the day you first knew. The rule counts discovery from the first day any employee other than the attacker knew, so a tech who saw something odd on a Friday and mentioned it on Monday has already spent three of the 30 days the FTC allows for a qualifying breach.

Systems and stores affected
Records involved, type and count

Covers 314.4(h)(2) and 314.4(j)(2).

6 Communications

Rumours move fast in a dealership. By lunch the service drive, the sales floor and a good number of your customers will have heard some version of what happened, so decide now who says what, and give your staff a short, calm script for the phone and the service desk. Customers who need a letter get one in plain English, and the FTC’s Data Breach Response guide includes a model letter to start from. Adapt it to your store. Floor plan lenders, your OEM and your DMS provider may have notice terms in their agreements, and those are far easier to find on a quiet Tuesday than in the middle of an incident. One person talks to reporters, and everyone else knows who that is.

7 Notification deadlines

A few notices run on hard deadlines, and the FTC’s is the one every covered dealership shares, at 30 days from discovery when unencrypted information on 500 or more consumers was taken. State laws add their own clocks and their own lists of who has to be told, and those depend on where your customers live, which is why a lawyer belongs in this part of the conversation. Don’t guess here. The notification sheet below puts it all on one page.

8 Recover and fix the weakness

Restoring from backup is what gets the sales floor working again, but it doesn’t close the door the attacker came through, and a store that skips that step can restore itself straight back into the same trouble. Put one name next to checking that the backups are clean before anything gets restored, another next to resetting passwords and multi-factor authentication, and a third next to confirming the hole is actually closed, whether it was a phished login, an old remote-access account or a server that missed a year of updates. Our notes on data backup best practices go deeper on the restore side.

9 Keep the record

Somebody should be writing things down from the first phone call to the all-clear, including the times, the decisions, who was called, what got unplugged and what it all cost. Your insurer will want that log, and so will anyone who later asks how you looked after customer information during the incident. A legal pad works fine. What doesn’t work is trying to rebuild the timeline from memory a week later, when everybody remembers it a little differently.

Covers 314.4(h)(6).

10 Review and update

Within a few weeks of any security event, big or small, get the people named in the plan back in a room to talk through what worked, what didn’t and what needs to change, then actually change the plan and put the new date on it. The rule asks for that review after an event. Keep the old versions. Between incidents we’d run a tabletop drill at least once a year, and again after anything that shakes the plan up, like a new store, a DMS conversion or somebody new in one of the named roles.

SECTION 7, UP CLOSE

Who a Dealership May Have to Notify After a Breach

Notices are where an otherwise good response tends to go wrong, and it’s almost always over dates. We’d fill this page in before you ever need it, ideally with your lawyer in the room, and keep it right behind the plan. Not every incident triggers every notice, so at the start of each one, go down the list and write a line about why each notice does or doesn’t apply.

The federal notice is the one with a fixed rule. Our guide to the Safeguards Rule breach notification walks through the FTC form field by field. It’s worth the read.

Notification sheet
Check each one at the start of an incident. Note why it applies or doesn’t.

FTC Federal Trade Commission

Due no later than 30 days after discovery when unencrypted customer information on at least 500 consumers was acquired without authorization. The notice goes through the FTC’s online form and covers who you are, what information was involved, the dates, how many consumers were affected and what happened. Don’t miss it.

16 CFR 314.4(j).

AG State attorneys general and residents

Each state where affected customers live can set its own deadline, its own threshold for telling the attorney general and its own rules for the customer letter. A dealer group near a state line can face two or three sets at once. Plan for that. Our guide to dealership privacy laws by state lists the clocks for the states we cover.

Check every state on the customer list.

INS Your cyber insurer

The notice clause in your policy sets this clock. Find it today. Some policies only pay for the forensics firms and lawyers the insurer has approved, so call the claims line early. Insurers look hard at preparation too, a point we covered in getting better cyber insurance terms.

Read the notice clause now.

LE Law enforcement

Report to the FBI through IC3 or your local field office. Keep the case number. If an official tells you in writing that public notice would hurt a criminal investigation, your FTC notice says so, and the rule lets law enforcement ask for a delay.

No fixed federal deadline.

BIZ Lenders, your OEM and your vendors

Floor plan lenders, captive finance companies, your manufacturer and your DMS provider can all have notice terms in their agreements. GM, for one, wants a confirmed incident reported within 72 hours, and our guide to OEM cybersecurity requirements covers the others. Find them now and write the deadlines on this page, so nobody is reading contracts at midnight.

Check each agreement.

SEC Publicly traded dealer groups

A public company discloses a material cybersecurity incident on Form 8-K, generally within four business days of deciding that the incident is material. That filing belongs to the group’s securities counsel, but the plan should say who tells them.

Form 8-K, Item 1.05.
