A Tool Raises the Alarm. Somebody Has to Answer It.

Cybercriminals don’t keep showroom hours. The phishing email lands on a Friday afternoon, and the part that matters, the moment someone uses that stolen login to wander toward the DMS, tends to happen when the building is dark. CDK Global’s research found 35% of auto dealers had a cyberattack or incident in 2024, and its 2023 study put the average downtime after one at 3.4 weeks. That’s three and a half weeks of deals written on paper. A tested backup and disaster recovery plan is what shortens it.

Managed detection and response is how Helion Technologies keeps someone watching during those hours, as part of cybersecurity for dealerships and run by the same team that handles your managed IT. If you’d like the definitions first, our MDR and EDR explainer covers them. This page is about the service, and what changes at your store when the alarm goes off.

HOW IT WORKS

One Alert, From Detection to Fix

Dealers ask us the same five questions about how managed detection and response works, usually in this order. The answers follow the life of one alert, from the moment something trips to the moment the hole is closed, and the point running through all of them is that a trained person makes the call while there’s still time for it to matter.

WHO DOES WHAT AT YOUR STORE?

Write it down before the night you need it. Our free incident response plan template covers who calls whom, and in what order. When an alert turns out to be real, incident response takes it from there.

What dealers ask about an alert
Five questions, in the order they come up

Detect “It’s probably nothing, right?”

Plenty of alerts are nothing. Say a PC behind the service counter kicks off a PowerShell script at 11:40 on a Saturday, with the building locked since six. That might be an update. It might be the fake parts invoice somebody opened on Friday, and the notification on your phone looks exactly the same either way.

The alert can’t tell you which one it is.

Triage “Can’t the software just handle it?”

Some of it. The software will block a known bad file on its own, no human needed. What it won’t do is notice that the same advisor’s Microsoft 365 login signed in from another state on Wednesday, or that two other PCs in the store started that script an hour ago. Our analyst connects those, and decides whether it’s real.

That decision is what you’re paying for.

Contain “Are you going to shut my store down?”

No. One PC comes off the network and the script gets stopped before it can reach the DMS, the F&I scanner share or the folder where accounting keeps payroll. On Monday the advisors log in at the next counter over.

The service drive opens on time.

Tell “Who’s going to tell me?”

We are. Your contact at the store hears which PC it was, what the script went after and what’s already been done, written so a general manager can read it without a glossary.

Names, times and actions, no jargon.

Fix “And who cleans it up?”

The same people your staff call about a printer jam. They rebuild the PC, reset every password that login could reach, find the email that started it and tighten the filter that let it through. A bolt-on security vendor would have sent a PDF.

One team, start to finish.

“We Have EDR” Isn’t the Same as Being Watched

Ask about security at most dealerships and somebody will tell you, usually within ten seconds, that they have EDR. Then we assess a store that isn’t a Helion client and find the same handful of things we always find, like computers that were never enrolled in the tool, agents that were installed and then stopped reporting in, default settings nobody touched and nobody at all reading the alerts. We wrote the pattern up in a dealership EDR issue.

Attackers know it, too. There’s now a whole class of tools built to switch endpoint protection off before the real attack starts, which we cover in EDR killers. Software that’s been quietly disabled doesn’t send an alert. Analysts watching the fleet notice when one machine goes silent, or when the tool and the behavior on that machine stop adding up, and that kind of thing is very hard to catch from a weekly report. Reports don’t notice silence.

The tool is half of it.

EDR on its own

Software on each computer that spots suspicious behavior and can stop some of it automatically. Somebody still has to enroll every device, tune it, read the alerts and act on them. Most stores don’t.

A smoke detector.

Managed detection and response

The same kind of tool, on every device and tuned for dealership systems, watched around the clock by live analysts who investigate what it finds and contain it.

Somebody answers it.

WHO’S CHECKING

Who Wants to Know Whether Anyone Is Watching

The first row is the one that costs you a sales month. The others ask the same question in their own paperwork, and a dealership with managed detection and response has a straight answer to all of them.

Who
	What they look for	Where managed detection and response fits
Cybercriminals	A machine nobody watches, a stolen login nobody questions and a weekend to work with	Somebody looks at the alert when it fires, while the intruder is still on one workstation
Cyber insurance underwriters	Endpoint detection and response is one of the three controls insurers recommend most, with MFA and privileged access management	A truthful yes when the renewal asks whether endpoints are covered and monitored
The FTC Safeguards Rule	Continuous monitoring of your systems, or else a yearly penetration test and vulnerability assessments every six months, plus logging of user activity	The continuous monitoring path, with logs that show what an intruder touched
Publicly traded dealer groups	A Form 8-K, generally due four business days after the company decides a cybersecurity incident is material	Facts about the incident early, while the clock is running

The insurance row comes from Marsh McLennan’s 2023 research on cybersecurity controls, which also notes those controls are commonly required by underwriters. The Safeguards row is in the FTC’s own Safeguards Rule FAQ for auto dealers and in 16 CFR 314.4, and the same FAQ requires you to tell the FTC within 30 days of discovering a breach involving 500 or more consumers, which we unpack in the Safeguards breach notification rule. The 8-K timing is from the SEC’s 2023 cybersecurity disclosure rules. The paperwork side of all this lives with our FTC Safeguards compliance team.
