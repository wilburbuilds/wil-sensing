A Scan Lists the Doors. A Tester Tries Them.

Ask a GM how many ways there are into the store’s network and you’ll get a guess. The real number is higher. A store collects them over the years, one vendor visit and one new building at a time. A vulnerability scan checks every one it can reach against a list of publicly known weaknesses. A penetration test is a person who takes what the scan found, and what it missed, and tries to get from the guest Wi-Fi to your customer files.

Both belong in cybersecurity for dealerships, because the attacks are real. CDK Global’s research found 35% of auto dealers had a cyberattack or incident in 2024. We test once a year, and a person does it. Our vulnerability monitoring runs twice a year, and both sit inside Helion Technologies’ compliance program. Want the textbook version first? Our explainer on vulnerability assessments and penetration testing covers it. This page is the service.

A scanner reports what it was told to look for. A person looks for what nobody thought of.

KNOW WHAT YOU’RE BUYING

Vulnerability Scan or Penetration Test? You Need Both.

The two get mixed up all the time, sometimes on purpose. They aren’t the same job. The table puts penetration testing for dealerships next to the scans that feed it.

Question
	Vulnerability scan	Human-led penetration test
Who does the work	Software, checking every system it can reach against a list of publicly known weaknesses	A skilled tester, starting from the scan results and using judgment to try to get in
How often the Safeguards Rule asks for it	At least every 6 months, and again after a material change to your operations	Once a year, scoped to the risks your risk assessment found that year
What it finds	Missing patches, open ports, outdated software and weak settings	Whether those small weaknesses chain together into a real path to customer data
What it can’t tell you	Whether anyone could actually use what it found	What happens next week, since it’s a close look at one moment in time
What you get back	A long list of findings, ranked by severity	How far the tester got, and which gaps to close first

Watch for automated scans sold as penetration tests. They cost less and the report looks official, but nobody tried anything. That’s why Helion’s annual testing is led by a person. A Pen Test Is a Snapshot. Monitoring Is the Alarm.

We’ve said this for years, and it still surprises people. A penetration test tells you how your network held up during the days somebody was testing it. If a cybercriminal gets in the week after the tester packs up, through a phishing email or a vendor’s stolen password, the report sitting in the controller’s office won’t do a thing to stop them. That’s also why the FTC Safeguards Rule lets a dealership skip the annual test and the 6-month scans only if it runs effective continuous monitoring instead.

Our advice is to have both. Around-the-clock monitoring, through managed detection and response, is what catches an attack while it’s happening, and our incident response team takes it from there. Testing and scanning are how you find the weak spots before anybody uses them, and how you check that the monitoring and your other safeguards really work. A scan nobody acts on is just a list. That’s the trouble with buying one on its own.

Testing finds the holes. Monitoring watches them.

THE COMPLIANCE SIDE

What the FTC Safeguards Rule Asks For

Testing sits in section 314.4(d) of the rule. At Helion it lives in the compliance program, next to the written risk assessment and vendor risk rating, and apart from security awareness training. We also built our own compliance scanning tool rather than reselling someone else’s. Here’s what the section says, without the legal phrasing.

FEWER THAN 5,000 CONSUMERS?

Section 314.6 exempts a dealership holding customer information on fewer than 5,000 consumers from the testing requirement. Count before you assume either way, and read our FTC Safeguards Rule checklist for the rest.

Section 314.4(d), clause by clause
What each part asks a dealership to do

(d)(1) Test or monitor what you’ve built

You have to regularly test or otherwise monitor whether your safeguards work, including the ones meant to spot an attack or an intrusion. Buying a firewall isn’t the finish line. Someone has to show it’s doing its job, and keep showing it.

Prove it works.

(d)(2)(i) A penetration test every year

Without effective continuous monitoring, you need annual penetration testing of your information systems, scoped each year to the risks your risk assessment identified. Added a store since last year? Moved to a new DMS? Then last year’s scope is out of date.

Scoped to this year’s risks.

(d)(2)(ii) Vulnerability scans every 6 months

Vulnerability assessments, including scans for publicly known weaknesses, at least every 6 months. The rule also wants one whenever there’s a material change to your operations or business arrangements, and whenever something happens that you know, or have reason to know, could seriously affect your information security program.

And after big changes.
