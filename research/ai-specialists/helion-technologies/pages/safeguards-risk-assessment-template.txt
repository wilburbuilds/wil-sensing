A risk assessment decides what the rest of the program does.

Ask a general manager where the dealership keeps customer information, and the answer usually starts and ends with the DMS. Then the controller brings up the credit bureau portal, the F&I director remembers the e-contracting platform, and somebody points out the boxes of old deal jackets sitting in the parts mezzanine. That conversation is most of a risk assessment. The rest is scoring. CDK Global’s 2024 study found that 35% of auto dealers had a cyberattack or incident that year, so the exercise isn’t theoretical.

This template walks through that conversation in ten sections, built on what section 314.4(b) of the FTC Safeguards Rule asks a written risk assessment to contain. It’s free. Change anything you like. It isn’t legal advice. The assessment is the base of your wider dealership cybersecurity program and your Safeguards compliance, because the safeguards, the testing and the training are all supposed to follow from what it finds.

One rule before you start. A risk nobody wrote down is a risk nobody owns.

Which Dealerships Need a Written Risk Assessment

Any dealership that arranges financing or leasing counts as a financial institution under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, so the FTC Safeguards Rule applies to it, whether it sells cars or heavy trucks and whether it runs one rooftop or twenty. Same test for everyone. The risk assessment sits in paragraph (b) of section 314.4. Everything else in the program is supposed to be built on top of it.

Size changes the paperwork, not the job. Under section 314.6 of the rule, a dealership that keeps customer information on fewer than 5,000 consumers doesn’t have to put its assessment in writing. It still has to base its safeguards on one, and an assessment that only lives in somebody’s head is a hard thing to hand over when a lender, an insurer or a new general manager asks for it.

A one-page version still beats a memory.

Fewer than 5,000 consumers

Writing the assessment down is optional for you, along with the written incident response plan, the board report and the fixed testing schedule. Basing your safeguards on an assessment of your risks is not optional.

A short version of this template is worth the hour.

5,000 consumers or more

The assessment has to be written and has to include the three sets of criteria in paragraph (b)(1). A store that has financed cars for a few years usually crosses this line once the old deal files are counted.

Fill in all ten sections.

IN THE RULE

What the FTC Safeguards Rule Requires in a Risk Assessment

Paragraph (b) of section 314.4 asks for seven things. The format is up to you. Here’s each one, what it looks like at a dealership, and where it sits in the template below.

What the rule asks for
	What it means at your store	Where it is in the template
Identify foreseeable risks, 314.4(b)	The inside and outside threats a dealership can reasonably see coming, from a phished F&I login to a breach at a vendor.	Sections 2 and 3
Assess the safeguards you have, 314.4(b)	Whether the controls already in place are enough for each risk, not only whether they exist.	Sections 5 and 6
Criteria for rating risks, 314.4(b)⁠(1)⁠(i)	Your own scale for how likely and how damaging a risk is, written down before anybody scores anything.	Section 4
Criteria for confidentiality, integrity and availability, 314.4(b)⁠(1)⁠(ii)	How you judge whether customer data stays private, stays accurate and stays reachable when the store needs it.	Section 5
Mitigate or accept, 314.4(b)⁠(1)⁠(iii)	What happens to each risk, who owns the fix, and who signed off on any risk you chose to live with.	Sections 7 and 9
Put it in writing, 314.4(b)⁠(1)	A dated document with your answers in it. Stores under 5,000 consumers are exempt from this part only.	The whole template
Reassess periodically, 314.4(b)⁠(2)	A fresh look at the risks and the safeguards on a schedule you set, and again after big changes.	Section 10

Wording from 16 CFR 314.4(b), as of October 2026. For the other eight elements, read the nine FTC Safeguards Rule requirements explained.

Risk assessment
Covers 16 CFR 314.4(b), (b)(1)(i) through (iii) and (b)(2). Owner is your Qualified Individual. Date it and keep every version.

1 Scope, owner and date

Start with the dull part, because an assessment with no date and no owner is hard to stand behind later. Name the stores and departments it covers, and name your Qualified Individual as the owner, since the rule puts that person in charge of the whole information security program. Then list who sat in the room. Names, not titles. A controller, an F&I director and a service director will each name risks the other two never think about.

Covers 314.4(b) and (b)(1).

2 Where customer information lives

Keep going past the DMS. The CRM holds customer information, and so do the F&I menu, the credit bureau and lender portals, email, the scanned deal jackets on the file server and the boxes of old deal files in the back. The rule’s definition of an information system even takes in phone systems that hold customer information, which matters if your BDC records its calls. Walk the building with a clipboard. You’ll find a shared folder or a filing cabinet nobody listed. Every store has one.

3 Threats you can see coming

The rule asks for reasonably foreseeable risks, inside the store and outside it, which is a sensible limit, since nobody is scoring meteor strikes. Outside threats at a dealership look like a phishing email built to steal an accounting login, ransomware on the file server, or a breach at a vendor that holds your data. The inside ones are quieter. They’re easier to miss. Think of shared logins in the F&I office, a salesperson who leaves and takes a CRM export along, or deal jackets left on a desk overnight. One line per threat is plenty.

Outside threats
Inside threats
Recent incidents or near misses

Covers 314.4(b).

4 Your scoring criteria

Decide how you’ll score before you score anything, or every risk ends up a medium. Keep the scale small. A three-point scale works for most stores, with likelihood running from unlikely to likely and impact from minor to severe, and the score is one multiplied by the other. Write down what each word means at your store. Severe might be any loss of customer records or more than a day without the DMS, while likely might be anything that has happened here, or at a store you know, in the last two years. 5 Confidentiality, integrity and availability

For each system on your list, ask whether the wrong person could see the data, whether someone could change it without anybody noticing, like a payoff amount or the bank details on a vendor invoice, and how long the store could run without it. That last one is availability. A 2023 CDK Global study put the average dealership downtime after an attack at 3.4 weeks, which is a long time to write repair orders by hand. Then check the controls you already have. Be honest about whether they’re enough.

7 Mitigate or accept

Every risk gets one of two decisions. Both get written down. Mitigate means a named person, a specific fix and a due date. Accept means someone with real authority signs the line and adds a sentence on why. That’s an owner or a general manager, not whoever was holding the pen. There are limits, though. Some safeguards are required however a risk scores, like multi-factor authentication and encryption of customer information, unless your Qualified Individual approves an equivalent control in writing.

Decision for each risk
Fix owner and due date
Accepted by, and why
Date signed

Covers 314.4(b)(1)(iii).

8 Vendors that hold your data

Your DMS provider, your CRM, the credit bureau connection, the e-contracting platform and your IT company all hold customer information or the keys to it, and the rule asks you to assess service providers based on the risk they present. Vendors do get hit. A 2025 ransomware attack on Motility Software Solutions, a DMS provider for truck dealers, ended with notification letters going to 766,670 people, and our write-up of the Motility DMS breach covers what dealers took from it. When CDK Global went down in June 2024, publicly traded dealer groups described the disruption in SEC filings. List every vendor, what it holds and when you last checked its safeguards. Ask for proof, not promises.

Vendor
What it holds or can reach
Security terms in the contract
Last review date

Covers 314.4(f)(3).

9 Where the results go

An assessment that sits in a drawer doesn’t do much, and the rule ties the rest of the program to it. Your safeguards are meant to control the risks it finds, the yearly penetration test is scoped from it if you test rather than monitor continuously, your vulnerability scans are based on it, your security awareness training gets updated to reflect it, and the Qualified Individual’s written report to the board or owner covers it. So this section is a short list of what changed because of the assessment. If the answer is nothing, that’s a finding too.

Pen test and scan scope
Training topics
Board or owner report date

Feeds 314.4(c), (d)(2), (e)(1) and (i).

10 Reassess

The rule says to reassess periodically and leaves the schedule to you. We’d do it at least once a year, and again whenever something big changes, like buying a store, a DMS conversion, a new vendor holding customer data, or a security event at your store or at one of your vendors. Keep every old version. Next year’s assessment goes faster with this year’s sitting right there, and the comparison shows anyone who asks that the program actually moves.

SECTION 4, UP CLOSE

How to Score a Risk at Your Dealership

Three levels of likelihood times three levels of impact gives every risk a score from 1 to 9. It’s crude on purpose. A room that argues for an hour over whether something is a 6 or a 7 has lost the point, which is getting the riskiest few things in front of the people who can pay for the fix.

Set the cut-offs first. Here’s how we’d draw them.

Likelihood down the side, impact across the top. Multiply them.

	Minor (1)	Serious (2)	Severe (3)
Likely (3)	3	6	9
Possible (2)	2	4	6
Unlikely (1)	1	2	3
6 or 9. Fix it, with a name and a date.
3 or 4. Fix it, or have the general manager sign off on living with it.
1 or 2. Accept it in writing and look again next year.

Sample risk register
Five risks we often see at dealerships, scored on the grid. Yours will differ.

9 A phished email login in accounting or F&I

Likely, because phishing reaches every store, and severe, because one mailbox can hold credit applications and wiring instructions. The existing control is multi-factor authentication on some accounts but not all of them. The decision is to mitigate, with MFA on every account by month end, owned by IT.

Likely, severe. Mitigate.

6 Ransomware on the file server with the scanned deal jackets

Possible, and severe. Nightly backups run, but nobody has tried a full restore since the server went in. The decision is to mitigate with a test restore this quarter and a backup copy kept offline, owned by the controller and IT together.

Possible, severe. Mitigate.

6 A breach at a vendor that holds customer data

Possible, and severe, since one vendor breach can reach every customer at once. The contract has no security terms in it. The decision is to mitigate by asking the vendor for its safeguards now and adding security terms at renewal. Our guide to evaluating security vendors lists the questions worth asking.

Possible, severe. Mitigate.

4 A departing salesperson exports the CRM

Possible, and serious. Logins get turned off when somebody remembers to do it. The decision is to mitigate with a same-day offboarding checklist that the office manager owns, starting with the next person who gives notice.

Possible, serious. Mitigate.

2 Deal jackets left on desks overnight

Unlikely, because the building is locked and alarmed after close, and serious if it ever happened. The decision is to accept, with a clean-desk reminder at the next sales meeting, signed off by the general manager.

Unlikely, serious. Accept in writing.
