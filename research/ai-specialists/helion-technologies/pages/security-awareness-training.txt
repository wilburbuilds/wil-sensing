Attacks on a Dealership Start at a Desk

The email that costs a store money rarely looks dangerous. It looks routine. It’s a parts vendor’s “updated remittance details” landing in the controller’s inbox on a Thursday afternoon, or a lead in the BDC queue with a PDF attached, or a text to a sales manager that seems to come from the GM. Business email compromise alone cost victims $3.05 billion in 2025, according to the FBI’s 2025 Internet Crime Report, and CDK Global’s research found 35% of auto dealers had a cyberattack or incident in 2024.

Security awareness training is the part of cybersecurity for dealerships that works on people instead of machines. Helion Technologies writes its own dealer-specific materials and runs the training as part of its managed security, with the same team that answers your help desk calls. If you’d like the background on why training belongs in a security program at all, our guide to employee cybersecurity awareness training covers it. This one is the service.

A firewall stops a lot. It can’t stop someone typing in their own password.

WHERE IT STARTS

Five Desks, Five Ways In

Generic training talks about “the finance department” and “your CEO.” A dealership has a controller, a BDC, a service drive and an F&I office, and cybercriminals know exactly which one to write to. They choose well. Lately that includes the marketing director, whose ad logins are the target of AI phishing lures aimed at dealership ad accounts. This is where our security awareness training for dealerships spends its time.

Desk
	What lands there	The habit that stops it
The controller and the accounting office	An email from a known vendor or the floor plan lender with new wiring instructions, often sent from that company’s real account after someone broke into it	Call the vendor back on the number already on file before a single bank detail changes
The BDC	Messages from strangers all day long, because that’s the job, including attachments and links that ask for a sign-in	Nothing that asks for a password gets typed into, and a strange lead gets reported, not deleted
Service advisors	A parts or warranty invoice nobody ordered, or a “customer” sending photos of the damage through a link	Match it to a repair order before opening anything
The F&I office	A lookalike lender or credit portal page asking them to sign in again	Open the portal from a saved bookmark, never from the email
Sales managers and the desk	A text or call that seems to come from the GM or the dealer principal, asking for gift cards or a quick favor before lunch	Confirm through a second channel, every time, no matter who it seems to be

These are the patterns that show up across the dealerships we support, written as the habits the training builds. They’re getting harder to spot, too. The same FBI report counts more than $30 million in 2025 losses to business email scams that used AI, which is how a fake vendor message ends up reading exactly like the real one. To see what an attacker could reach after one of those clicks, our complimentary Hackability Test shows it from the inside.

Written for Dealers, by Helion’s Own Team

Here’s what usually happens with an off-the-shelf course. It was built for every industry at once, so the bad emails in it come from a bank or a hospital. Your advisors click through the videos on a slow afternoon, pass the quiz and go back to the counter. A week later a parts invoice turns up from a supplier nobody’s heard of, and nobody connects the two. Why would they? Helion’s team writes its own material for dealerships so that connection gets made. Erik Nachbahr, our founder and president, says it the way he’d say it to a dealer: “it’s specifically tailored to dealers. Like, it’s dealer-specific.”

The examples are a controller’s inbox, a BDC queue, a repair order and a lender portal, because that’s where your people work. That’s deliberate. It also has to stay current. The FTC Safeguards Rule asks for training that’s updated as needed to reflect the risks your own risk assessment finds at your stores, so when the scams change, the material changes too.

THE NEWEST MODULE

AI Use Belongs in the Training Now

Your salespeople are already using AI to write follow-up emails and tidy up deal notes, and a lot of it happens on personal accounts nobody at the store can see. That’s new territory. So our training now covers AI use alongside phishing. For stores that want a sanctioned platform instead of a ban, that’s the job of Managed AI.

“WE’RE NOT SEEING DEALERS DOING ANYTHING WITH AI SECURITY TRAINING.”

Erik Nachbahr, Founder & President, on an October 2026 webinar about AI governance. Watch the clip or read AI governance for dealerships.

What the AI module covers
Three questions staff ask, and the answers we teach

Accounts “Can I just use my own ChatGPT?”

Not on a dealership computer, and not for dealership work. A personal account has no audit trail and no off switch. Nobody can shut it down. If a salesperson uploads their customer list to one, that list walks out the door with them on their last day, and the store never knew it was there.

Company work goes in a company account.

Uploads “What’s the harm in having it check one email?”

That one email has a customer’s name and phone number in it, and maybe a payoff amount. Paste it into an AI tool the store doesn’t control and that information now sits somewhere you can’t see or delete. You can’t take it back. We teach a plain rule, which is that nothing about a customer goes into an AI the dealership doesn’t manage.

If it’s about a customer, it stays in.

Training setting “Doesn’t it forget what I typed?”

Often it doesn’t. Unless someone has switched it off, a lot of AI tools keep conversations and use what you give them to train the model, so it can surface for somebody else later. A corporate platform lets the store turn that off for everyone at once. Staff learn to check which kind of account they’re in before they type.

Know where your words go.
