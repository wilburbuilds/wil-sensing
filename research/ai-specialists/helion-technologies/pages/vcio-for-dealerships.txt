vCIO for Dealerships: Strategic Technology Leadership for Dealer Groups

A technology plan, a budget and vendor decisions your dealer principal and CFO can actually read, without hiring a full-time CIO.

Ten Vendors, Twelve Rooftops, Nobody Owning the Plan

Every dealer group we assess looks about the same. There are ten or more vendors on the books, an internal IT person or a small team keeping the stores running, and nobody whose job it is to decide what the technology should look like two years from now. We’ve already seen that exact setup across 2,000+ dealerships. Different rooftops, same story. Big groups fix it by hiring a chief information officer, but most groups don’t have one, and plenty shouldn’t, because the CIO work at a dealer group comes in bursts around budget season, an acquisition, a DMS decision or a contract that’s about to renew.

That’s the job a vCIO, or virtual CIO, does part-time from outside the building. Our services page calls it Strategic Technology Leadership, and it belongs to managed IT for dealerships. This page covers that one piece, meaning who owns the plan, what goes in it, and how it fits around the IT people you already have.

The stores get run every day. The plan usually doesn’t. That’s the gap.

Your IT Manager Isn’t the Problem. Time Is.

Picture a Saturday. The finance office printer jams with a buyer waiting, a salesperson can’t get into the DMS, a technician’s tablet won’t connect in the back bay, and the same person is supposed to be pricing next year’s PC replacements and reading a phone contract that auto-renews in March. The tickets win. They always do, because the store is open and somebody is standing right there. Erik Nachbahr, who founded Helion in 1997, says internal IT teams “tend to not have time” for “the strategic stuff, the planning,” and that’s how we frame it every time, as a time problem and not a talent problem.

We say it that way on purpose, since plenty of dealer group IT managers are very good at their jobs. Replacing them isn’t the fix. On the co-managed path your IT manager keeps the stores, the people and the daily calls while we carry the planning, the budget work and the vendor reviews, or you split it the other way around. Our co-managed responsibility matrix writes down who owns what.

Brandon Dorsey, CISSP, Helion’s Vice President of Technology Strategy, leads this side of our work, which is how Helion helps dealer groups modernize their technology and reduce risk.

Different job. Usually the same desk.

The tickets come first

A store that’s open has people waiting, so the strategy work gets pushed to next week, and then to the week after that.

Every single time.

A second set of eyes

Erik puts the question to leadership plainly: do you want a second look at how your money’s getting spent? A lot of owners honestly don’t know.

Asking isn’t an insult.

When one person leaves

One group that split from eight stores to three lost its whole internal IT team overnight, CIO included, and everything they knew left the same day.

Plans shouldn’t live in one head.

STRATEGIC TECHNOLOGY LEADERSHIP

What a Dealership Technology Plan Puts on One Page

Ask most groups for their technology plan and you’ll get a folder of vendor invoices, if that. Erik is blunt about it. Dealerships mostly don’t have IT budgets, so the first thing we do is gather everything they’re spending money on and bring it all together. Once it’s in one place, these are the six lines we keep current for a dealer group, sized to however many rooftops you run.

BUILDING NEXT YEAR’S NUMBERS?

Our dealership IT budget checklist lists the line items dealer groups most often miss, from the second internet circuit to the Safeguards testing.

The technology plan
Six lines a vCIO keeps current for a dealer group

Spend Every IT, phone and security invoice

Start with the bills. A typical group pays a DMS provider, a phone company, an internet carrier or two, a copier lease, an outside IT shop, a security vendor and a handful of subscriptions somebody signed up for years ago, and the controller is often the only person who sees all of them. It adds up. We line every one up by store and by category. That’s usually where the first savings show up, because overlap is easy to miss when the invoices come from ten directions.

Every invoice, one table.

Renewals Contracts and their end dates

Phone systems, circuits, copiers, software and the DMS itself all come with terms, notice windows and, quite often, automatic renewals. Those are sneaky. Miss a notice date and a group can end up locked into another term on a deal nobody would sign today. The plan keeps every end date on one calendar with a reminder well ahead of the notice window, so you negotiate on your schedule instead of the vendor’s.

Know the date before the vendor calls.

Refresh PCs, servers and network gear by age

Hardware gets old on a schedule, so it should get replaced on one. Windows 10 proves it. Microsoft ended support on October 14, 2025, and its Extended Security Updates program charges organizations $61 per device for the first year, with the price doubling every year after that for up to three years. A store that skipped the planning pays it on every old PC, and pays more for each year it waits.

Replace on a calendar, not a breakdown.

Projects The next store, a relocation or a DMS move

Growth is where a plan earns its keep. One 23-store group in New England went through four dealership acquisitions and ten store relocations with Helion behind it, and IT never became the bottleneck. The work starts before closing with a look at the target store’s network, DMS setup, Microsoft 365 and security, which our page on dealership acquisition IT walks through phase by phase. A platform change gets the same early treatment through DMS transition.

Priced before it’s promised.

Risk Security, and the report owners actually read

A breach stops the sales floor, and that’s the real cost. The plan names who watches for attacks, how backups get tested and what the compliance program covers. If your stores arrange financing, the FTC Safeguards Rule also expects your Qualified Individual to report in writing, at least once a year, to the board or owners on the security program, and that written report is part of the work Helion’s compliance program carries.

Owners see it in writing.

AI Which AI tools staff are allowed to use

Dealers are buying AI licenses faster than anyone is writing down what they’re for. Order matters here. Our view is that AI decisions start with leadership, which at a smaller store means the dealer principal or the CFO, and that approved platforms and goals come before the licenses go out. Give staff an approved path and they have far less reason to paste customer data into a personal account. Helion’s Managed AI is that path, and our AI governance guide covers the policy side.

Plan first, licenses second.
