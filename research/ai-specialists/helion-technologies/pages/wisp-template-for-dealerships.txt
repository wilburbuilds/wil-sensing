A WISP is the binder the rest of your security program lives in.

Ask a dealership to show its information security program and you’ll usually get a stack. There’s the IT company’s onboarding packet, an MFA policy somebody emailed around a couple of years ago, a vendor list on the controller’s laptop, and maybe last year’s pen test report. Most of the work exists. Nobody has tied it together. That’s the job of a written information security program, or WISP, and section 314.3(a) of the FTC Safeguards Rule lets it be written “in one or more readily accessible parts,” so a cover document with tabs behind it is fine.

This template is that cover document. Its 13 sections follow the order of section 314.4, and each one says what to write and which separate part it points to. It’s free. Change anything you like. It isn’t legal advice. It sits under our wider dealership cybersecurity work and our FTC Safeguards compliance program, and CDK Global’s 2024 study found that 35% of auto dealers had a cyberattack or incident that year, so the binder gets used. For the background on what the rule means by a comprehensive information security program, our earlier explainer covers it.

One rule before you start. If it isn’t written down, it isn’t the program yet.

THE TEMPLATE

The Dealership WISP Template

Thirteen sections, in the order the rule lists them. Most stores can write a first draft in an afternoon with the controller, the F&I director and whoever runs IT at the table. Where a section points to a separate part, like the risk assessment or the incident response plan, file that part right behind it.

Rather work offline? Download the editable Word version or the printable PDF. Both carry the same 13 sections, a parts index and a signature block.

Written information security program
Covers 16 CFR 314.3 and 314.4(a) through (j). Owner is your Qualified Individual. Date it, sign it and keep every version.

1 Purpose, scope and objectives

Say what the program protects and where. List every rooftop, department and entity it covers, including a body shop or a separately owned finance company if they share systems with the store. Then copy in the three objectives from section 314.3(b), which come down to keeping customer information confidential, protecting it from threats you can anticipate, and stopping access that could harm a customer. Short is fine. Vague isn’t.

Customer information in scope
Program objectives
Effective date and version

Covers 314.3(a) and (b).

2 Qualified Individual and oversight

Name one person to oversee, implement and enforce the program. The rule calls that person the Qualified Individual, and it can be an employee, an affiliate or a service provider. If you use an outside provider, three things follow. The dealership still owns compliance, a senior person at the store directs and oversees the Qualified Individual, and the provider has to keep its own program that protects you. Write all three down. Lenders and insurers ask.

Senior person who oversees them

Covers 314.4(a).

3 Risk assessment

Point to your written risk assessment here, with its date and the few risks that scored highest. Our risk assessment template covers the rest.

Points to the risk assessment. Covers 314.4(b).

4 Access controls and MFA

Write down how people get in and what they can see once they’re in. A salesperson doesn’t need the accounting module, and a porter doesn’t need the CRM. Then the rule gets specific. Multi-factor authentication is required for anyone accessing any information system, unless the Qualified Individual approves an equivalent or stronger control in writing. Note who reviews access, how often, and what happens on somebody’s last day.

How access is granted and removed
MFA coverage and written exceptions

Covers 314.4(c)(1) and (c)(5).

5 Data, devices and systems

List the data, people, devices, systems and rooms the store runs on, ranked by how much they matter to the business. At most dealerships that means the DMS, the CRM, the F&I and e-contracting platforms, the credit bureau and lender portals, Microsoft 365, the file server, every laptop and phone, and the back room where the paper deal jackets sit. Keep the full list as its own part. Update it when something changes.

Covers 314.4(c)(2).

6 Encryption

Customer information has to be encrypted at rest and in transit over external networks. Where that truly isn’t feasible, an older system for example, the rule allows compensating controls, but only ones your Qualified Individual reviews and approves. List each exception, the control that covers it and who signed off. An empty list is the goal.

Covers 314.4(c)(3).

7 Applications, changes and monitoring

Three short paragraphs belong here. The first covers how you check the security of software you buy, and of anything built in-house, like a custom trade-in form on the website. The second covers how changes to systems get approved and recorded before anybody makes them. The third covers how you log what authorized users do, so somebody notices when a login starts exporting the whole customer list at 2 a.m., which is usually where managed detection and response comes in.

How purchased and in-house apps are checked
Change approval process
Activity logging and who reviews it

Covers 314.4(c)(4), (c)(7) and (c)(8).

8 Retention and disposal

Customer information has to be disposed of securely no later than two years after you last used it to serve that customer. The exceptions are records you need for the business, records the law makes you keep, and data stored in a way that makes targeted disposal not reasonably feasible. Dealerships keep a lot on purpose, so write down which records you keep and why. Then say how paper gets shredded and how old drives get wiped. Old deal files pile up fast.

Records kept and the reason
Disposal method for paper
Disposal method for drives and devices
Last retention policy review

Covers 314.4(c)(6).

9 Testing and monitoring

Say how you check that the safeguards actually work. Without effective continuous monitoring, the rule asks for a penetration test every year, scoped from the risk assessment, and vulnerability assessments at least every six months and after material changes. Record the dates. Note who ran each one and where the results went. A test nobody acted on is half a test.

Continuous monitoring in place, and what
Last penetration test and next
Last vulnerability assessment and next
Where results go

Covers 314.4(d).

10 People and training

Everyone gets security awareness training that changes when your risks do. The rule also expects qualified security staff, in-house or through a provider, who get their own updates and keep current on threats. Write down the training schedule, how you track who finished it and who your security people are. Keep the attendance records behind this tab.

Training topics and schedule
Completion tracking
Security staff or provider
How they stay current

Covers 314.4(e).

11 Service providers

Your DMS provider, the CRM, the credit bureau connection, the e-contracting platform, payroll and your IT company all hold customer information or the keys to it. The rule asks you to pick providers that can protect it, require safeguards in their contracts and assess them periodically based on the risk they present. When CDK Global went down in June 2024, publicly traded dealer groups described the disruption in SEC filings. Keep the vendor register as its own part, with a review date on every row. Ask for proof, not promises.

Covers 314.4(f).

12 Incident response and FTC notice

Point to your written incident response plan, which has to cover seven areas, from its goals and roles to communications and what you change afterward. Our incident response plan template covers all seven. Then add the deadline the plan can’t miss. If a notification event involves the information of at least 500 consumers, you notify the FTC as soon as possible and no later than 30 days after discovery, on the form on its website.

Points to the incident response plan. Covers 314.4(h) and (j).

13 Annual report and program review

At least once a year the Qualified Individual reports in writing to the board, or to a senior officer if there’s no board. The report covers the program’s overall status and compliance, plus material matters like risk decisions, vendor arrangements, test results, security events and recommended changes. The program then gets adjusted after testing, after each new risk assessment, and after material changes like buying a store. Date every version. Keep the old ones.

Covers 314.4(g) and (i).

Which Dealerships Need a Written Information Security Program

Any dealership that arranges financing or leasing for its customers is a financial institution under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, so the FTC Safeguards Rule applies, whether it sells cars or heavy trucks and whether it runs one rooftop or twenty. Same test for everyone. Our page on the Safeguards Rule for heavy truck dealerships covers that side of the business. The FTC says the same in its own Safeguards Rule FAQ for automobile dealers.

Size changes some of the paperwork. Under section 314.6 of the rule, a dealership holding customer information on fewer than 5,000 consumers is excused from four items, the written risk assessment, the fixed testing schedule, the written incident response plan and the annual report to the board. The program itself still has to be written. Section 314.3(a) has no size exemption.

Small stores still need the binder. It’s just thinner.

Fewer than 5,000 consumers

The WISP is still required, in writing. Sections 3, 9, 12 and 13 can be shorter, because the written risk assessment, the testing schedule in (d)(2), the written incident response plan and the board report don’t apply to you. MFA, encryption and vendor oversight still do.

Fill in every section, even the ones that end up a line long.

5,000 consumers or more

Every element applies in full, including the written risk assessment and incident response plan, the yearly penetration test, scans twice a year and the Qualified Individual’s written annual report. A store that has financed cars for a few years usually crosses this line once the old deal files are counted.

Fill in all 13 sections and file the parts behind them.

BEHIND THE COVER DOCUMENT

The Parts of a Dealership WISP

The rule lets the program be written in one or more readily accessible parts. Here’s how we’d lay out the binder, what goes behind each tab and where to start on it. One tab per part.

Part of the WISP
	What goes in it	Where to start
The cover document	Scope, the Qualified Individual, your safeguards and who owns each part. Sections 1 to 13 above.	This template
Risk assessment, 314.4(b)	Every foreseeable risk to customer information, scored, with a decision to fix it or accept it.	Risk assessment template
Inventory, 314.4(c)⁠(2)	Data, devices, systems and rooms, ranked by how much the business depends on them.	Section 2 of the risk assessment template
Access and MFA standard, 314.4(c)⁠(1), (c)⁠(5)	Who gets access to what, where MFA is on, and any exception the Qualified Individual approved in writing.	Our guide to MFA
Vendor register, 314.4(f)	Every provider that holds or reaches customer data, its contract terms and its last review.	Vendor evaluation guide
Testing and training records, 314.4(d), (e)	Penetration test and scan reports, what got fixed, and who finished training.	Penetration testing
Incident response plan, 314.4(h)	The seven areas the rule lists, plus the FTC notice for events involving 500 or more consumers.	Incident response plan template
Annual report, 314.4(i)	The Qualified Individual’s written report to the board or owner, and the changes it led to.	FTC Safeguards Rule checklist
