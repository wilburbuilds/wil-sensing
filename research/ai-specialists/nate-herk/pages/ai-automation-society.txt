What If AI Should Choose the Decision Before Choosing the Action?
An agent receives a task and immediately reaches for a tool. But what if the tool call is already the wrong decision? Before acting, an agent should determine: → Outcome: What actually needs to change? → Options: Act, gather more information, ask a human, or do nothing? → Trade-offs: Which option best balances risk, cost, and reliability? → Commit: Execute only when the choice meets defined constraints. This separates the decision layer from the execution layer. The agent doesn't just ask, “What can I do next?” It asks, “Which decision is worth executing, and what evidence would justify it?” In your workflows, should the decision layer belong to the LLM, deterministic rules, or a combination of both?

The October Railway breach hit my Google and Anthropic AI keys this week: the 4-step recovery checklist I ran
This week my Google and Anthropic AI keys got hit in the wake of the October Railway security incident, even though our own repositories had zero public leaks. On October 23, Google Cloud deactivated the Gemini API key for my photo app (pFrame) after a sudden spike of 13,740 requests ($47.81 in usage before rate limits and automatic suspension cut it off). Two days earlier on October 21, our Anthropic account was hit with a roughly $90 single-day spike ($88.14 across 12 rapid auto-recharge invoices in a few hours) before we disabled the exposed keys, which we are currently appealing with Anthropic. I immediately ran a full exposure audit across 290 local Git histories, 17 Netlify sites, 10 Vercel projects, and all 34 public JavaScript bundles on pFrame. Our code was clean. Then I saw the security alert from Railway, where several of our back-end services run. Between October 20 and October 22, attackers breached Railway's internal systems and decrypted stored environment variables across a subset of workspaces. Railway reported that the attackers used uncensored AI models in a targeted campaign to harvest AI provider keys ("LLMjacking") and crypto wallets. Because our Google key sat inside Railway before the breach, and Railway warned us that our project names matched the attacker's AI-key search pattern, I treated all 79 distinct secrets across our 13 Railway projects (102 variable bindings) as compromised and started rotating them right away. Even if your own repo security is tight, a host or supply-chain breach (like the TeamPCP attacks on LiteLLM and Axios this year) can still expose your keys. Here is the 4-step disaster recovery checklist I now run across every AI project: 1. Put a hard dollar cap on every AI provider account Git hooks do not help if a cloud host gets breached at 2 AM. Set a hard monthly spend limit and disable uncapped auto-recharge inside Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic so a stolen key shuts off before it turns into a four-figure invoice.

Can't come up with enough work to keep your AI agent busy? How I fixed the 90-second prompt bottleneck
Models have gotten so good at understanding problems and executing that work that used to take hours now finishes in 10 to 15 minutes. Lately I kept hitting the same wall: as soon as I stepped away from my desk, my coding agent was already done and waiting on me. The AI was no longer the bottleneck—I was. When I audited 1,402 of my interactive Claude Code sessions (4,367 prompts) over a two-week window, the numbers showed why: - 74% of my regular chat runs finished in under 5 minutes, and 92% finished in under 15 minutes. - The median active run from a standard prompt was just 90 seconds before the agent stopped and waited for me. - 82% of my prompts had no automated pass/fail verifier, and 99.8% did not include a finished reference example. On Friday I used my autonomous task loop (goal-loop) to break that bottleneck. I pointed it at my project memory (OpenBrain) to scan my projects in flight, pick the 5 highest-impact tasks, build and verify them, and repeat for 12 hours. It finished 14 waves (70 verified loops across 20 repos) while I worked on other things, plus a 4-hour continuous improvement run on my home-repair app (moHelp). Across my logs, goal-loop runs lasted 17x longer than regular chat prompts. Even then, my old goal-loop had a flaw: it started coding right away from a one-sentence prompt with an 8-pass cap. Whenever my intent was fuzzy, 42% of those runs still parked early to ask me clarifying questions. Yesterday I upgraded the workflow by combining an upfront interview skill (grill-me) with "role engineering" patterns from Nick Saraev and Anthropic's engineering team: 1. Have the agent interview you before it touches a file. Instead of guessing from a one-line prompt, my upgraded loop runs a 5-question upfront interview (grill-me) in two minutes at my desk. Lock a deterministic pass/fail verifier and one gold-standard example.
