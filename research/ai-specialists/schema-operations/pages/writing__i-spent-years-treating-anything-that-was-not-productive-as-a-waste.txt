I spent years treating anything that was not productive as a waste

The military trained me to treat non-productive time as a liability. The company gave me an endless supply of productive time. What changed once the decisions finally had other owners.

Written by
Martin Toomey
Published
16 December 2025
Reading time
4 min read

Share on X
All writing

Years in the military jaded me toward relationships, religion, and anything that did not have a clear output. The work had a mission. Everything else was secondary or a distraction. When I got out I kept the same standard. I started working seven days a week, minimum fourteen-hour days. If it did not move the company forward it did not make the schedule.

I told myself the hours were temporary. The company was new. The decisions still sat with me. Pricing, scope, cash, the customer who wanted the owner—none of it had another name on it yet. The days filled. The weeks became a sequence of tasks. I was productive. I also could not point to a repeated moment that existed for any reason other than the work.

The jaded part made it easy to justify. Relationships required time that did not produce a result I could measure. Religion felt like something I had already set down. Rest was what you did when the mission was finished, and the mission was not finished. I ate in the truck. I took calls on Saturday because the thread was still open. I told people I would get to the other things once the company could run without me in every decision. The company could not run without me in every decision because I had never written the boundaries that would let someone else make them.

I noticed it when I realized I had not done anything in months that was not instrumental. No walk without the phone. No meal that was not standing up or between sites. No hour that existed because it was worth doing even if it did not move a number. The military had trained me to treat non-productive time as a liability. The company had given me an endless supply of productive time because every real yes still came through me.

I started writing the decisions down. The three defaults for a scope change. The dollar amount on pricing that no longer needed me. The two situations that still required my yes and everything else that did not. I named the owners. I meant it when the first ones felt slower. The fourteen-hour days thinned. The seven-day weeks stopped being required.

Once the hours were no longer fully claimed, I started keeping a small set of rituals at home. Nothing public. A set time in the morning to read and pray before I opened anything related to the business. The same short prayers at night. One meal a day at the table with no phone. The order stayed the same. Nothing about it moved a deal or cleared a thread. That was the point. It interrupted the default I had been running since I got out—the assumption that every block of time needed a measurable result or it was waste.

The benefit showed up in small places first. I stopped treating the early morning as recoverable work time. The conversations at home got longer because I was not already half in the next task. I slept better, not because the practice fixed anything on its own, but because the week now contained repeated moments that were not instrumental. The judgment I used during the day was sharper. I was less likely to take the borderline deal just to keep the motion going. The rituals did not make me more productive in the way I used to measure it. They kept the rest of the week from becoming only logistics.

I still work. I still make the calls that actually need the standard I carry. I also walk most mornings without the phone. I eat at least one meal a day at a table. The morning reading and the night prayers stay on the schedule. Those things do not grow revenue. They are the first repeated moments in years that are not for the company.

The military taught me to stay on mission. It did not teach me what to do when the mission became the only thing the week contained. The company kept moving once other people could use the standard. I got back a small number of hours that were not productive in the way I had defined it, including the ones that belong to a ritual kept at home. Most of the owners I work with are running a version of the same pattern. The weeks are full. The moments that are not for the business have already gone to zero. They usually notice when someone asks what they did that was not work and the answer takes too long.
