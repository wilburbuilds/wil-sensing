Scorecards

One page, seven to ten numbers, one named owner, green, yellow or red. Weekly scorecards fail because the founder still holds the yes. Install them like an operating design, not a ritual.

Written by
Martin Toomey
Published
6 January 2026
Reading time
5 min read

Share on X
All writing

You do not need software. You need owners, numbers, and a meeting that does not turn into therapy.

Weekly scorecards fail for one reason: the founder still holds the yes, so the page becomes a performance for you. Implement them like an operating design, not a ritual.

What a weekly scorecard is

One page. Seven to ten numbers. One named owner. Green, yellow, or red against a target you already agreed. No paragraph until the color is on the page. If they need a deck to explain the week, they do not have a scorecard. They have a story.

Green means the number is on target. Keep going.
Yellow means it is slipping. Act this week before it becomes a fire.
Red means the system missed. Name the cause and the move that does not require you.

Yellow is where founders lie. They treat it like "almost fine" and wait. Yellow is the warning. Red is the autopsy.

What it is not

A dashboard you glance at in the car. A CRM export. A "team update." A place to hide behind pipeline adjectives. If the number cannot be wrong in public, it is decoration.

The four pages at $2–5M

Marketing. Sales. Delivery. Finance. That is the set. Not twenty pages. Not a leadership page so you can supervise your own bottleneck and call it culture.

If you add a fifth page this early, you are usually avoiding one of these four.

How you actually install it

Write the numbers before you hire a tool.

Marketing owns demand you can count: spend, leads, cost per lead, qualified leads handed to sales, show rate, speed to first response, channel that actually produced the qualified ones. Vanity reach is not a scorecard. If sales says the leads are junk and marketing says volume is up, you do not have alignment. You have two stories.

Sales owns conversion: conversations, qualified opportunities, proposals, closed cash, days to close, win rate, stalled deals older than your rule. Pipeline "looks healthy" is not a color. A number is. Real estate version is the same math: calls, conversations, appointments, signed listings.

Delivery owns the promise: started on time, finished on time, rework, margin versus bid, hours versus estimate, issues older than 48 hours. If the customer got the thing late and nobody owned the date, that was you. Still.

Finance owns the oxygen: cash on hand, collected this week, AR over 30/60/90, weeks of payroll, gross margin, cash in versus cash out. Finance is not "we'll check QuickBooks on Friday." If cash is yellow and everyone is celebrating sales green, you are about to learn an expensive lesson.

Give each page one throat to choke. Not "the team." A name. If two people own it, you own it. Helpers without decision rights will bring you a yellow and wait. That is not accountability. That is messaging.

Set the bands in writing. Green, yellow, red. Not vibes. A lead number that is 10% under target is yellow. 20% under is red. Pick the bands. Lock them. If you move the bands so nobody feels bad, you built a mood board.

Run the same 30 minutes every week. Same day. Same time. Owner reads the page out loud. Green. Yellow. Red. One sentence on yellow and red: what they will do in the next seven days that does not require your blessing. Then you shut up.

Your only job in that meeting is to notice where the system still routes back to you. If every fix is "I'll talk to Martin," the scorecard is working. It just told you the design is still you.

Publish it. Shared sheet is fine. Whiteboard is fine. Hidden tabs are how founders keep control and call it "not ready." If the company cannot see the color, the owner cannot be responsible for it.

Do not add a number because it is interesting. Add it because someone can act on it this week without asking you.

The first four weeks will be ugly

Week one, the numbers will be late, incomplete, or fiction. That is useful. You just found out you do not have a system. You have memory and heroics.

Week two, people will argue definitions. Good. Lock them. A "lead" is not a form fill from a tire kicker. A "conversation" is a live talk with a qualified buyer. "On time" is the date you promised the customer, not the date you privately hoped.

Week three, someone will try to skip because "it was a weird week." That is the week you needed the page. Weird weeks are when the business tells the truth.

Week four, you will want to jump in and save a red. Do not. If you save it, you taught them the scorecard is optional and you are still the system. Yellow gets a plan. Red gets a plan with an owner. You do not get to be the plan.

Rules that keep it from rotting

Yellow is not a participation trophy. Yellow without a next action becomes red on purpose.

Red is not a character flaw. Red without an owner and a move is.

Marketing green and sales red means you bought noise. Sales green and delivery red means you sold work you cannot finish. Delivery green and finance red means you were busy going broke. The pages talk to each other. If you read them one at a time like report cards, you will miss the company.

If they cannot move the number without your yes, rewrite the rule and give them the right. Discount band. Spend limit. Date change. Hire. Write it. Then stop being the filter.

Fire the metric nobody uses to make a decision. Keep the one that changes behavior by Friday.

Do not let marketing own sales close rate. Do not let sales own cash. Do not let yourself own all four because "nobody else gets it." That sentence is the stall.

When the pages are honest and the company still needs you in every exception, you do not have a metrics problem. You have an authority problem.

Weekly scorecards do not make you a professional manager. They make the bottleneck visible. Once it is visible, you either give the yes away or you admit you wanted a job with a nicer title.
