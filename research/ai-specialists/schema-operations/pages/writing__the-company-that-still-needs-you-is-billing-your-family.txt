The company that still needs you is billing your family

The company that still needs you in every room bills the people who cannot fire you. Relationships erode the same way the business stalls: one decision at a time that still requires you.

Written by
Martin Toomey
Published
25 July 2026
Reading time
4 min read

Share on X
All writing

The company that still needs you in every room is the one that bills the people who cannot fire you.

Your kid stopped coming into the room when you were on the phone. You called that focus. It was the company training your family that the founder is present in the house and unavailable in it.

Most owners in the $2–15M range do not lose the relationships in one dramatic moment. They erode them the same way the business stalls: one decision at a time that still requires them. The partner gets the leftover twenty minutes, and those twenty minutes are still half a Slack thread. The kid learns to wait or stop asking. The friend who would have told you the thing you did not want to hear stops calling because the last three times you were "in the middle of something." You notice it later, usually when something breaks and no one is in the room who knows the real version of you.

I have sat across from owners who can name the exact close they took from the parking lot of a recital. They remember the call. They do not always remember what the kid was doing when they walked back inside. The company needed the answer. The people who cannot fire you absorbed the cost. That is not a work-life balance problem. That is an unwritten rule that every room still needs your face.

One owner ran a $6.4 million specialty trade business. He was proud he never missed a client call. Then his wife asked him, after a normal Tuesday, whether he had heard their twelve-year-old say he did not want to play baseball anymore. He had not. He had been on the phone in the next room approving a change order that the project lead could have owned. The kid had stopped bringing him the real thing months earlier. The change order still sat with him because no one else had the written right to move it. The family paid for a decision the company had not yet transferred. We wrote the three defaults for a finished change order and named who could approve anything under the cap without him. He still took the calls that actually needed him. He stopped taking the ones that only needed a yes he had already defined.

Another founder told me his marriage was "fine, just busy." They had stopped having conversations that were not logistics. The last real argument had been about a weekend he canceled because a key client wanted a status call on Saturday. He took the call. The client was not going to leave. The partner had already learned that the calendar won. Six months later the same client still needed him on routine updates. The marriage did not get the version of him that existed before the queue became the operating system. We put the actual emergencies on one page—cash, legal, safety, or a major account about to break—and sent everything else back with a named owner. The Saturday calls dropped. The conversations at home stopped being only logistics.

Relationships of the kind that actually matter require unglamorous repetition. Showing up when it is inconvenient. Remembering the detail that is not in a status report. Being willing to let a thread wait because the person in the room cannot be replaced by a later debrief. Most founders treat this as a scheduling problem. It is usually a design problem. If the company still stops when you are not available, then every evening, every weekend, and every hospital visit is still on the company clock. The people who would sit with you in the hospital are the ones currently subsidizing the version of the business that cannot run without you.

You can keep the original reason you started the company and still give the people who cannot fire you a version of you that is not half in a thread. That requires the same move the business requires: naming who owns the calls that currently pull you out of the room, writing the boundary so someone else can use it, and meaning it when the first one feels slower. We do that work with owners. We take the decisions that still pull you out of the room—price, scope, cash, the "got a minute" that is not actually urgent—and put the standard and the owner on one page so the company can keep moving when you are not in it. The multiple does not change overnight. The evenings do. The alternative is a company that bills the people who would notice if you disappeared, and a set of relationships that exist on the calendar but not in the room. Most owners do not run that math until someone else points at the empty chair.
