The four pillars of a life that actually matters

Meaningful work, relationships, fitness and ritual. Not a manifesto — a page in a notebook about keeping four load-bearing walls from quietly decaying.

Written by
Martin Toomey
Published
4 July 2026
Reading time
8 min read

Share on X
All writing

Most people do not collapse because one big thing goes wrong. They erode quietly. A job that pays well but feels empty. Relationships that exist on calendars but not in the room. A body treated like an afterthought until it starts sending invoices. A sense of meaning that only appears on holidays or in a crisis.

I like to write things in a notebook to organise my thoughts before I turn to a computer to polish them. The page this came from is crude but honest. Four lines. No manifesto. Just a reminder that a life needs load-bearing walls, not décor.

These four pillars — meaningful work, relationships, fitness, and spirituality — are not original. Versions of them appear in Stoicism, in religious traditions, in modern psychology, and in the quiet conversations people have after someone they know burns out or dies too young. What makes the list useful is its lack of romance. I'm not promising transformation in thirty days. I'm asking you to keep four things from decaying at the same time.

Meaningful work: why you started, not the money

Work occupies more waking hours than almost anything else. If those hours are spent only extracting a paycheck, the rest of life has to compensate, and it usually cannot.

"Why you started" is the useful test. Most people begin out of pragmatism, or even desperation. The lucky ones stumble onto a path with some mix of curiosity, competence, and the desire to build something of lasting value. Money arrives later and is a necessity. But it is a terrible north star.

Meaningful work does not require a calling that sounds good at dinner parties.

A plumber who takes pride in clean joints and honest pricing is doing meaningful work.
A teacher who still cares whether the third kid in the back row understood the lesson is doing it.
A founder who remembers the original problem they wanted to solve, not just the valuation, is doing it.

The opposite is also common: people who once loved the craft and now only love the title, the equity, or the ability to leave early on Fridays.

The practical move is periodic reconnection with the original reason. Not vision-board language. Actual memory. What problem felt worth solving? What skill felt worth getting good at? When the answer becomes "so I can afford the next thing," the pillar is already cracking. Money is a result and a constraint. It is a poor purpose.

People who keep the original "why" visible tend to make better long-term decisions about clients, hours, and what they will and will not tolerate. They also recover faster when a project fails, because their identity was never only the outcome.

This pillar does not demand that every Tuesday feel transcendent. It demands that the overall direction still makes sense when you subtract the salary.

Relationships: family and friends, not a network

The note specifies family and friends. That is a deliberate narrowing. Professional networks, group chats, and "we should get coffee sometime" acquaintances are useful. They are not the same category.

The people who will sit with you in a hospital waiting room, or tell you the thing you do not want to hear, are a smaller set, and they require maintenance that feels inefficient.

Family can be the hardest version, because you do not choose the starting conditions. Friends are the chosen version, which makes neglect easier to justify. Both decay the same way: infrequent contact that is always "busy right now," conversations that stay at the surface because going deeper takes time neither person has scheduled, and the slow replacement of presence with updates. Life adds its own distance too. People grow, change, move forward or slip back, and the gap opens without anyone choosing it.

Social media makes this look like connection. It is usually broadcasting.

The research on loneliness and mortality is not surprising. Isolation predicts worse health outcomes at a scale comparable to smoking or obesity. What the studies cannot fully capture is the daily burden: the person who has no one to call when something goes wrong, or when it goes amazingly well, or the person whose only conversations are transactional.

Relationships of the kind listed on the page require unglamorous repetition — showing up, remembering details, being willing to be inconvenienced.

This pillar also includes the willingness to prune. Not every family tie is healthy. Not every old friendship still fits. The point is not volume. It is a small number of people with whom the relationship is real enough that both sides would notice if it disappeared.

Fitness: you are not a bodybuilder

The part in parentheses is the most useful part: "You're not a bodybuilder."

Fitness culture sells extremes because extremes photograph well. Most people do not need a program designed for someone whose job is hypertrophy. They need a body that can carry groceries, play with kids, sit through a long day without collapsing, and still work in thirty or forty years.

The mistake is treating fitness as either an identity or an afterthought. The identity version produces an accumulation of fatigue, an obsessive calendar built around training blocks, and a likelihood of injury. The afterthought version produces the slow accumulation of stiffness, extra weight, poor sleep, and the moment a doctor mentions numbers that used to belong to "old people."

The middle path is unglamorous: consistent movement, basic strength, some conditioning, and enough protein and sleep that the body can actually recover.

You do not need a six-pack. You need to be able to get off the floor without it becoming a project. You need joints that do not announce the weather. And the muscle you build in your thirties is what means that, in your forties and beyond, a bad stretch of illness or stress does not erase years of capacity. Walking, lifting something heavy a few times a week, and not eating like a teenager forever will outperform most complicated programs that last six weeks.

This pillar is also the most visible when it fails. Work and relationships can be papered over for a while. A body that cannot keep up announces itself.

The note's reminder is a mercy: do not turn health into a second career. Just don't abandon it.

Spirituality: especially the rituals

Spirituality is the easiest pillar to leave vague, which is why the note adds "especially rituals."

Belief systems differ. The human need for something that is not only utility does not. Rituals are the practical expression of that need. They create rhythm in a life that otherwise becomes an endless sequence of tasks and notifications.

Ritual can be religious in the traditional sense: prayer, liturgy, Sabbath, fasting, pilgrimage. It can also be secular but still structured: a daily walk without headphones, a morning page of writing that is not for anyone else, a meal that is not eaten standing up, a regular visit to a place that is older or quieter than your ordinary surroundings.

The common feature is repetition that is not optimized for output.

Rituals work because they interrupt the default. They force a pause that the rest of the week will not give you. They also connect the individual life to something larger — community, tradition, nature, or simply the fact that you are not the first person to wonder what any of this is for.

Without some version of this, people often try to extract meaning exclusively from work or relationships, which overloads those pillars until they break. You do not have to resolve the metaphysical questions. You do have to decide whether your weeks will contain any moments that are not instrumental.

Holding the four together

The four pillars are not independent. Neglect one and the others absorb the stress until they cannot.

A person who only has work will use relationships as a support system rather than a good in themselves.
A person who only has relationships and no work that matters often becomes restless or financially fragile.
A person who ignores the body eventually has less energy for the other three.
A person with no ritual or inner life treats every setback as purely personal and every success as insufficient.

Balance here does not mean equal hours. It means none of the four is allowed to go to zero for long.

The notebook page is useful precisely because it is short enough to remember when life gets loud:

Meaningful work that still has a "why."
A few relationships that are actual.
A body treated like it has to last.
Rituals that keep the rest from becoming only logistics.

Most advice fails because it asks for a new personality. This asks for maintenance. It is a page in a notebook, not a published book, and that is the right scale. You do not need a perfect system. You need four things that do not quietly disappear while you are busy with everything else.
