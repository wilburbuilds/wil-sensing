Twice the profit, three times the value

Two companies, same work. One can run without the founder and one cannot. A buyer pays three times as much for the first, and the multiple is set long before you decide to sell.

Written by
Martin Toomey
Published
16 August 2026
Reading time
4 min read

Share on X
All writing

Acompany that does $4 million in profit and can run without the founder is often worth three times a company that does $2 million and cannot. Most owners never run that math until a buyer does.

Two companies. Same work. Similar people. Similar market.

The first does $2 million in profit. The founder is still in the big calls, the key client relationships, the pricing exceptions, the questions that actually move money. A buyer looks at it and offers roughly four times profit. Eight million, maybe. They are buying a job that happens to have employees.

The second does $4 million in profit. Other people own the numbers that matter. The founder can leave for a month and the work that generates cash still happens. A buyer looks at that and offers roughly six times profit. Twenty-four million. Twice the profit, three times the value.

I have run companies on both sides of that line. The four-times version feels like control. The six-times version feels like you gave something up. The difference shows up in what still requires you after the conversation ends.

Marcus, Elena and David are composites, drawn from more than one company, with names and details changed.

Marcus runs a $5.2 million commercial HVAC company. Utilization looked fine. Cash was tight. He spent most of the previous month rewriting proposals and jumping on client calls because "they want to talk to the owner." Change orders over $1,500 and any invoice past 45 days still needed his approval. The jobs were getting done. $180,000 sat in unbilled work and unchased invoices because the two people who owned those numbers were waiting on him. He was busy. The company was not solvent in the way a buyer would recognize. We wrote the three defaults for what a finished change order looked like and named who could approve anything under the cap without him. Within three weeks the unbilled number started moving and he stopped rewriting scopes at night.

Elena owns a $7 million professional services firm. She stepped out of the sales process for six weeks to focus on delivery. Pipeline volume stayed about the same. Close rate dropped from 28% to 17%. Her team followed the written steps and used the CRM. They did not have the filter she used when a prospect started asking for custom work that would destroy margin. Deals that she would have walked away from in the first call moved forward. She came back to a fuller pipeline of work that was going to lose money. We sat down and wrote the two or three signals she actually used to kill a deal early, then put them where the team already worked. She stayed out of most first calls. Close rate recovered without her having to re-enter every conversation.

David had already written the pricing bands for his $3.8 million specialty contractor. Under 4% the salesperson could discount without asking. Between 4% and 8% the sales lead owned it. Anything higher came to him. Margin still slipped two points over a quarter. The exceptions were logged in a shared doc. No one reviewed them. The sales team treated the bands as a starting suggestion and waited to see if David would notice. He usually did, three or four weeks later, after the jobs were already underway. We added one line to the weekly numbers: total discount dollars given that week and who approved them. The review took ten minutes. The pattern became visible while the jobs were still open instead of after they were done.

Most owners treat the multiple as a future problem. Something to fix when they are ready to sell, or when they are tired, or when the next hire is supposed to take it off their plate. By then the multiple has already been set by the way the company currently runs. The buyer is not paying for the potential version of the business. They are paying for the version that exists when they look at the last two years of decisions and see who still had to be involved.

We take the three or four decisions that still move price, scope, or cash and put the actual standard and the owner on one page. In the shops where that sticks, the number of threads that still require the founder drops within a few weeks. The multiple does not change overnight. The conditions that produce the higher multiple start to exist.
