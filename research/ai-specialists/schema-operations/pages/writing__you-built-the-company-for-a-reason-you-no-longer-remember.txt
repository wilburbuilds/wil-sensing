You built the company for a reason you no longer remember

Most founders can still describe the original problem they set out to solve. They cannot remember the last time it drove a decision. Feelings do not transfer. Standards do.

Written by
Martin Toomey
Published
28 January 2026
Reading time
3 min read

Share on X
All writing

Most founders in the $2–15M range can still describe the original problem they wanted to solve. They just cannot remember the last time that problem drove a decision instead of the next thing that needed their yes.

The work that built the company usually started with something specific. A trade you were good at. A client type no one else wanted to serve cleanly. A margin you could see that others treated as normal. Over time the calendar filled with the decisions that still sat with you. Pricing exceptions. Scope changes. The hire that needed your read. The cash question that could not wait. The original reason did not disappear. It just stopped being the filter. Money and motion replaced it because those were the things that still required you in the room.

One owner ran a $4.8 million electrical contracting business. He started it because he was tired of watching general contractors treat good electricians as interchangeable. He built a reputation for clean work and honest change orders. Three years later he was spending most of his week approving discounts and rewriting proposals so the jobs would close. The reputation was still there. The decisions that protected it were not. He could still name the original standard. He could not point to the last time it had overridden a short-term yes. We wrote the three things that still made a job worth taking—the margin floor, the client type, the scope that stayed inside the original offer—and named who could walk away from anything that failed them. He stopped rewriting the proposals that violated the standard. The work that came in looked more like the company he had meant to build.

Another founder had a $9 million specialty manufacturing shop. She started it to solve a lead-time problem her previous employer treated as normal. Customers paid for speed and reliability. Five years later she was in every pricing conversation and every major scheduling call. The lead times had stretched. The reason she had started the company was no longer visible in the decisions the team made when she was not there. We put the original lead-time standard and the exceptions that were allowed on one page, then named who owned the schedule when it was threatened. She stayed in the conversations that actually changed the model. She left the ones that only needed the standard she had already set. The lead times started moving back toward the reason the company existed.

Most owners treat the original reason as something they will get back to once the company is running without them. The company does not run without them precisely because the original reason was never written down as a decision someone else could use. It stayed in their head as a feeling. Feelings do not transfer. Standards do.

You can still make the calls that only you should make and give the rest of the company the original filter so they can make the rest without you. We take the two or three standards that still define why the work is worth doing—the margin, the client, the offer, the lead time—and put them on paper with a named owner and a clear boundary. The company keeps the reason you started it. You stop being the only person who can still apply it. The alternative is a business that grew past the problem you set out to solve and a founder who can describe the original why but cannot find it in last month's decisions. Most owners notice the gap when someone asks them what the company is actually for and the answer takes longer than it used to.
