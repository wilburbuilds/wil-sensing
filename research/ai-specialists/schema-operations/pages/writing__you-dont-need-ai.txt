You don't need AI. You need competent people running a clear system.

AI can help a person who already knows the standard, the boundaries and the outcome they own. It cannot supply the lines. Until the system exists, the tool mostly feeds the same inbox.

Written by
Martin Toomey
Published
28 September 2026
Reading time
3 min read

Share on X
All writing

Most of the owners I talk to are being told the next advantage is AI. Better proposals. Faster customer answers. Fewer people required. The pitch treats the constraint as speed or headcount. In the companies that are actually stuck between a few million and the next level, the constraint is usually simpler: competent people still do not have a clear system they can run without bringing the real questions back to the founder.

AI can help a person who already knows the standard, the boundaries, and the outcome they own. It can draft, summarize, or surface options inside those lines. It cannot supply the lines. When the system is missing, the tool produces faster versions of work that still need the same review, the same exception handling, and the same yes that already fill the week. The team gets a new input. The founder keeps the bottleneck. Output volume may rise. Decision volume that reaches the owner stays roughly the same.

One owner of a $3.1 million specialty print and fulfillment shop had already tested two AI tools—one for quoting, one for routine customer emails. The drafts were often usable. Every quote that involved a non-standard material, a timeline under five days, or a customer with a pattern of late changes still landed in his inbox for a final look. The estimator saved time on the first pass. The owner did not save time on the decisions that mattered. Customer emails that touched pricing or scope followed the same path: generated quickly, then held for his edit. We sat down and wrote the three job types the team could quote and schedule without him, the specific dollar and timeline thresholds that still required a review, and the person who owned the handoff when a job crossed the line. The same people then used the AI tool inside those boundaries. Standard quotes moved without him. The exceptions that still needed his read were fewer and clearer. Turnaround on the work the team owned improved. The queue that reached him shrank because the system, not the tool, determined what was allowed to stand.

The pattern repeats across shops that have added software, scorecards, or now AI without first naming ownership. The new layer makes activity more visible and sometimes faster. It does not move the point at which work stops and waits. Competent people running a clear system will outperform a founder who is still the final filter, whether or not those people also use AI. The system states what finished work looks like, who can approve the routine cases, and which situations still come back. AI, when it is used, becomes one optional input those people can apply to speed their own portion of the work. It does not replace the need for someone other than the founder to own the outcome.

Most companies currently chasing the tool are still missing the system the tool would need in order to change anything important. The advantage is not the software. It is competent people who can run the work to a defined standard without creating a thread for every judgment call. Once that exists, any tool—including AI—has a chance to help. Until it exists, the tool mainly helps the team generate more items for the same inbox.
