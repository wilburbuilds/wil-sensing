## The Google Business Profile Build.

The single biggest lever in local SEO, and where we start on every new client. Read a step, then watch it done live in the video.

STEP 1

### Lock the primary category.

* **One category, and it follows your revenue**, not what sounds impressive.
* Some trades barely have a choice. A plumber picks Plumber and moves on.
* HVAC is where it gets interesting, because there are plenty of categories to pick from.
* Split revenue between heating and cooling, and want to rank for both? **HVAC Contractor.**
* Mostly furnaces, heaters, and heat pumps? **Heating Contractor.**
* Mostly AC repairs, installs, and replacements? **Air Conditioning Repair Service.**

STEP 2

### Add secondary categories.

* Fill every slot that genuinely fits. The HVAC shop above runs four secondaries behind the primary.
* A plumber adds drain cleaning, water heater installation, sewer line repair, emergency plumber.
* **Relevant only. No stuffing.**

A real HVAC category build

One revenue-aligned primary, four real secondaries. Nothing stuffed.

STEP 3

### Build out every service, with descriptions.

* **Every single service you offer gets its own entry** and a real description.
* Mention what Google expects to see around that service. Equipment, problems solved, related terms, your city.
* Match the language on your profile to the language on your website.
* Don't write 20 of these by hand. Run the prompt:

searches). Format it as a table I can work down while editing my profile.

STEP 4

### Settle the storefront question.

* **A physical address beats a service-area profile every time**, in almost every market.
* The check: search your keyword, count how many of the top 10 run a real storefront. That tells you what your market requires.
* Small-town exception: if nobody in the top 10 has an office, a service-area profile can still win.

STEP 5

### Add a realistic service area.

* List the towns, cities, and zips you actually serve, and **do this even if you have a physical-location profile.** The two aren't either/or: the address anchors your pin, the service area tells Google everywhere else you work.
* The one hard rule: **everything you list needs to be within about a two-hour drive** of your location.
* Keep it realistic. The areas your trucks actually roll to, not a whole state pasted in.

STEP 6

### Add products.

* Load your main services as products, with pricing context. Starting-at numbers or ranges.
* Photos of your real jobs work a little better here if you have them. Ideally one for every service you offer.
* Same play as the services section. Don't write these by hand:

Copy this prompt into an LLM (ChatGPT, Claude, etc.) Copy prompt

Notice the output is different from the services prompt. **Products want prices and photos.**

STEP 7

### The name. (The Exact Match Name Strategy.)

* **Google gives relevance credit to names that match what people search.**
* You'll see tiny profiles named just "\[City\] AC Repair" with no brand attached. That's a profile built to game the system, and it tells you the signal works.
* The real-brand version: your brand plus your primary keyword. Johnson's AC Repair.
* Want more? Add one or two keywords, not five. Johnson's Heating, Cooling, and Plumbing Repair. Stuffing reads as spam and gets flagged.
* **The line you do not cross: file a real DBA for the new name first.** Faking it gets the name reverted or the profile suspended.
* One contractor runs this for his two main services and outranks shops with four times his reviews.

✕ THE FLAG

### The advanced version: two verticals, two profiles.

* Common problem: your shop runs two verticals, say plumbing and HVAC, and your profile only ranks for one of them. We see it all the time.
* **The fix:** a second profile, with a real DBA, optimized entirely around the second vertical's keywords. The original stays focused on the first.
* **The cost:** reviews don't transfer. The new profile rebuilds them from zero, which is exactly what the review engine in the next pillar is for.
* **The payoff:** two profiles, each ranking hard for its own lane, instead of one profile splitting the difference.

STEP 8

### Set 24/7 hours, and actually answer.

* **After-hours calls are the emergencies, and emergencies are the best tickets.** Set your hours to 24/7 if you can actually answer.
* Tommy Mello stands in front of entire rooms of contractors and tells them the same thing: without 24/7 hours and someone answering the phone, a person or an AI agent, you're missing about 30% of your leads.
* And a huge share of homeowners search for this stuff between 6 and 10 at night, scrolling on the couch, finally off work.
* Bigger shops can support around-the-clock answering. Smaller shops usually can't, and most never run the math on what that gap costs.
* **The rule either way: never post hours you don't answer.** A missed call teaches Google, and the customer, not to send the next one.

STEP 9

### Opening date equals founding date.

* Operating since 1998 with a profile created in 2021? **Set the date to 1998.**
* Aged profiles with consistent history outrank new ones. This is accuracy, not a trick. Google asks when the business opened, not when the profile did.

### The ongoing work. (What "optimized" means month to month.)

* **New photos every month.** Team, trucks, finished jobs. Authentic, high quality, geotagged. Google has never seen your job photos before, and that's the point.
* **Reply to every review.** Mention the service and the city in the reply.
* **Posts: we still recommend them, for the right reason.**
* We've tested posts across dozens of clients, and they don't move rankings in any real way. Don't pay anyone for posts as a ranking strategy.
* Do them anyway. An active profile is an activity signal, and the customer comparing three shops picks the one that looks alive.
* Posts also pick up a second job later in this doc: the Learn More button can point at your BBB and Yelp pages to keep your citations indexed.

## The Review Velocity System.

Look at the profiles that sit at the top of the Map Pack and pull the most calls in any market. Almost every one of them is also getting the most reviews each month. That is not a coincidence. **It's one of the biggest needle movers in this entire system.**

60/mo

20/mo

A shop doing **60 reviews a month** typically outranks a shop doing 20\. Velocity, not total, is the metric almost nobody tracks.

### Which review numbers matter, in order.

1

**Velocity.** How many you're generating right now. A profile stacking reviews this month beats a bigger total that went quiet six months ago. Recency is the metric almost nobody tracks.

2

**What the review says.** "Five stars" is fine. "ABC Plumbing cleared our drain in Metairie same day" ranks you for the service and the city.

3

**Who leaves it.** Reviews from Local Guide accounts carry extra weight.

4

**Your rating.** The Map Pack is full of 4.7s and up.

5

**Total count.** The thing everyone obsesses over. It's fifth for a reason.

6

**Photos in reviews.** Customer photos get pinned and trusted. If your work is visible, ask for the picture.

### The system that produces velocity.

* **The ask happens in person, before the tech leaves the job site.** Highest-converting request that exists.
* Make it part of closing the job, not an optional extra. The biggest operators are moving to mandatory: no close-out without the ask.
* We've live trained sales teams at eight-figure home service brands on this exact ask.

The ask script

"If you were happy with the work, it would mean a lot if you left us a review. You can scan this QR code, it just takes 20 seconds."

* **QR code card in every tech's hand and every truck.** Scanning to review takes 30 seconds.
* **The photo play:** the best companies we work with train techs to have the customer take a photo of the tech in front of the new system, sometimes with the customer in it. People see those reviews, and it builds your brand and authority more than anything else can.
* **Pay for it. $25 per review.** One shop went from 5 reviews a month to 35 on that incentive alone.
* Stack a competition on top of the cash. Top three techs every week or every month, depending on how many jobs your shop runs.
* Have good prizes:

Paid date night with the wife Flat screen TV PS5 for the kids Cash 

* Now the part nobody wants to hear. **The system is easy. The training is the work.**
* A tech won't go out of his way for $25 if the ask isn't second nature. You drill it until it is.
* And it's bigger than a script. It's culture. **Do good work, then get the review that proves it.** In the shops that win, that sentence is the job description.

### The backstop, when the tech doesn't get it.

* **Automation runs the follow-up.** Text at 12 hours, email at 24 if there's still no review.
* At 48 hours, it becomes a phone call, and that call belongs to your CSRs.
* Give the office the same deal: $25 per review they recover, with their own competitions.
* The setup is one spreadsheet: every customer from the trailing seven days who hasn't left a review. CSRs work the list.
* For a CSR, $25 a review is awesome money for what's already their job: calling customers and taking care of them.
* Owner calls only make sense in a small shop. Past that, this belongs to the team.
* **The ramp rule: don't jump from 2 a month to 30 overnight.** Google filters spikes. Build toward a steady monthly number.
* One more reason this matters: reviews are no longer just a Google Maps play. AI engines read them across every platform. That's Pillar 4.

## The Website.

Your website and your profile are one system, not two projects. Google reads the site behind the GBP to decide how high that profile deserves to rank, so the site is a big part of why you rank in the first place, and it's what keeps you there. It's also what turns the click into a booked job. **We build it in seven parts.**

PART A

### The bridge. Where your profile's website button points.

* Every GBP has a website button, and where it points matters more than most agencies realize.
* **One location:** point it at a homepage fully optimized for your service and your city.
* **Multiple locations:** each profile points at its own city page. Never all of them at the homepage.
* The reason: one homepage cannot be optimized for the same keyword in three different cities. Each profile needs a landing page built for its own area.
* **Remember this rule. It comes back in the multi-location playbook.**

PART B

### Match the site to the profile. (The on-page basics.)

Google is reading the same business from two places, your profile and your website, and checking that they tell the same story. Most of on-page SEO for a home service company is exactly that: **your GBP says what you do. Your website confirms it, page by page.**

PART D

### The sitemap. Service pages, then staged location pages.

Home Page Services Locations About Us Photos / Our Work Plumbing Drain Cleaning Water Heater Repair Sewer Line Repair Scottsdale Phoenix Glendale Mesa 

Service pages link to their related location pages, and back. Clean internal links, no orphan pages.

* The structure: Homepage → one thorough page per service → one real page per city you serve. Clean internal links, no orphan pages.
* The old version of this course taught building 50 to 100 location pages out of the gate. **We were wrong, and the data made it clear.**
* What actually works is staging it.
* **Stage 1:** 8 to 10 deep location pages for your core cities. Google reads 50 near-identical pages as duplicate content and ranks none of them.
* **Stage 2:** once those rank, expand into service-location pages. Not "roofing company in Saratoga County" but "metal roofing in Saratoga Springs." That's where the buyer with a specific job is searching, and almost none of your competitors have the page.

### What makes a location page real instead of thin.

* Answers to local questions with local pricing. "What does a water heater cost in Austin? Usually $1,200 to $2,500 installed."
* Content about the actual place. Neighborhoods, common home types, the problems your trade sees there.
* Photos and reviews from jobs in that specific area.
* A map embed plus driving directions from nearby towns. It reads as helpful to Google because it is helpful.
* The next level, and a few of the larger shops we work with run this: tools that automatically push new job photos and reviews from that area's GBP onto that area's location page.
* Google and the language models read that as live, trustworthy, local proof. Fresh photos and fresh reviews from real jobs in that zip, updating on their own. **It tells them this company does good work, and does it there.**

### Where the local information comes from.

How to find information your competitors don't have

* Local Reddit threads: questions and complaints about the service in that city or neighborhood.
* Google, forums, Facebook groups, community boards, the questions people keep asking.
* Interview the owner and the techs: what changes from one part of the metro to another?
* Real jobs completed in that city: common problems, unusual installs, home ages, equipment, project costs, permitting, access issues, before-and-afters.
* Customer interviews from that area: why they called, what surprised them, what the job involved.
* CRM and job data, but only when it ties back to a specific zip, city, or job address.

Don't ask Claude to rewrite the five pages already ranking. **Give Google and AI information that could only come from a company that actually works there.**

## Citations and Links.

Most of what agencies bill under this section is filler. Here's the part that matters and the part you can skip.

### Citations. (The foundation, not the win.)

* A citation is your name, address, and phone number listed somewhere Google trusts.
* **The era of 500 junk directory submissions is dead.** There's a core that actually matters:

YelpAI-CRITICAL BBBAI-CRITICAL Angi Thumbtack Facebook Apple Maps Your trade's big directories 

* **BBB deserves its own line.** It's one of the strongest trust signals on the internet, and the AI engines read it too. You'll see it again in the next pillar.
* After the core, go local and targeted: your chamber, county and city directories, local business associations.
* Finding them is simple. Search "small business directory in \[YOUR CITY\]" and work the results. Or run the prompt:

Copy this prompt into an LLM (ChatGPT, Claude, etc.) Copy prompt 

I run a [TRADE] company in [CITY, STATE]. Build me a list of 50 real
citation opportunities: the major national directories every local business
needs, the top industry directories for [TRADE], and local directories,
chambers, and business associations for my city, county, and state. For
each one: name, URL, free or paid, and a priority ranking. Skip low-quality
spam directories. Format as a table I can paste into a spreadsheet.

* **The rule that matters more than the count: NAP identical everywhere**, down to the suite number and the abbreviations.
* Moved offices? Updating your GBP is not enough. The old address lives on in dozens of directories, and the mismatch is a trust killer.
* Multiple locations get treated as separate businesses. Unique NAP per location, its own citations.
* $10 Fiverr packages hurt more than they help.

### The part nobody checks: are your citations even indexed?

* **If your citation pages aren't indexed on Google, they're pointless.** They do nothing for you.
* The common version: a company runs Bright Local, submits 75 citations, and only 10 or 15 ever get indexed. You paid for 75\. You think you have 75.
* The fix is linking. Strategically link out from blog articles to your citation pages.
* Even GBP posts help here: the Learn More button pointing at your BBB or Yelp page keeps those primary citations crawled and indexed.
* **Indexed citations are the only ones that show up, and showing up is the entire point.** That's where the trust and authority come from.

### Backlinks. (Where the trust actually comes from.)

* A backlink is another website vouching for yours.
* **The high-value sources for a local shop:** your suppliers and vendors, trade associations, your chamber (worth it under $500 a year), local news, sponsorships, press releases.

The partnership pitch that works

"I'll send customers your way, let's link to each other's sites."

Pitch it to service businesses in your market that don't compete with you.

* Evaluate any link on four things:

Is the site relevant? Does it get real traffic? Is it a clean, real website? What's the domain rating? 

### The sponsorship play, executed.

* Don't search for "places to sponsor." **Look for local organizations and events actively asking for sponsors**, then check whether the website is worth getting a link from.
* Where to look:

Local charitiesYouth sports leaguesFestivals & racesNeighborhood organizationsSchools & universitiesIndustry eventsNonprofitsCommunity events 

* Run the same four checks above on the organization's site before you pay.
* A few hundred dollars for a permanent link from a strong local site is often a very good buy. **Good backlinks frequently cost money, "just earn them naturally" is not a strategy.**

## Tracking: How You Know It's Working.

What you measure, you can improve. Shops that guess where leads come from stay stuck, and they keep paying for marketing that doesn't work.

* **The setup:** a unique tracking number on your GBP, and dynamic number insertion on your website so the displayed number swaps by traffic source while your real number stays consistent everywhere it's printed.
* Every lead source lands in the CRM, and revenue gets attributed back to the channel that produced it. **A phone number becomes a dollar amount.**
* Watch the trend lines too: organic traffic in Search Console or Ahrefs, and a monthly geo-grid scan in Local Dominator so you can see your map coverage change.

### The two numbers that decide everything.

LTV : CAC

**The long-term number.** Everything a customer spends with you over the years they stay, repeat jobs, memberships, referrals, divided by what it cost to acquire them. **This is the number that tells you whether to spend more, not less.**

ROAS

**The short-term number.** Return on ad spend: what a campaign collected right away, divided by what it cost. $10K in jobs on $2K of spend is a 5x ROAS. Run it on anything paid.

Same denominator, different horizon: ROAS grades what the spend got you right now. LTV:CAC grades the whole relationship. A job that only breaks even on ROAS can still be a big win on LTV:CAC.

**When those two are healthy and your radius is won, you don't add more SEO to the same profile. You add a location.**
