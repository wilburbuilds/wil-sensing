## Page 2: How we work

### Intro
**Lightweight and fast.**

We find the jobs worth handing off, however many apply. Each one gets a measure and a baseline, and the work is sized to that set. The first list is never the last.

### The four steps

**1. Fit call.** Free. 30 minutes. No commitment. We talk about how the work runs and where the hand work piles up. It ends in a Job map, a no and why, or not yet.

**2. Job map.** Free, and short. No six-month study and no slide deck. We try to find every job worth handing off. There is no set number. Each measurable job gets: Improve [measure] from [baseline] to [target] by [date]. If we can't write that sentence, the job stays off the build. You keep the map: the jobs, the measures and baselines, what the system does and what a person still does, and the order to build them. It is a starting list. New jobs show up later.

**3. Build.** The first paid step. The price is sized to the jobs on the map. Retire what isn't needed. Plain automation where the work has to be exact. AI only for messy input, like emails, notes, and photos. Tested on real work before it's trusted. How much it does on its own matches the stakes. Something new is written down: handled inside the job, agreed as a change before going on, or added to the list. The build stays on what was scoped.

**4. Run and govern.** Monthly, and you can stop. We watch each job against its measure, report it, and review the list: what's new, what changed, and what's next. A job that keeps passing can take on more. New jobs from the list get a short map of their own.

### A job is a fit when
- Someone owns it and can tell when the work is right.
- We can get at the records and systems.
- How much it does on its own matches the stakes, and it earns more by passing tests.
- No rules get bent.
- We can write a measure and a baseline. If we can't, it stays on the list and out of the build.

## Page 3: Our Work

### Intro
**Real work Wil has built.** The businesses stay unnamed.

The four examples below are peers. The shop is not the main example, and the others are not secondary.

### A local auto repair shop
Wil was hired full-time there for business development and marketing. Here's what he found and built there.

### Vendor credits
Wil matched 11,139 purchase-order lines against billing and found vendor credits that weren't coming back. The leak stopped going forward. A person still checks the exceptions. **$217K. Found, not recovered.**

### Parts pricing
Pricing each part instead of a blanket markup, in a 90-day model. The tool only flags. A person approves before anything changes. **About 48% higher gross profit on eligible parts. Modeled, not yet realized.**

### What was built
- One trusted record of customers, cars, orders, calls, and history.
- Caller screen before anyone picks up.
- Morning shop brief every weekday.
- Customer follow-up list.
- Newsletter in the owner's voice.
- Knowledge kept, with no outcome figures: front-office chat sessions summarized into repeatable skills, and a technician research tool that returns step-by-step answers with sources.

### How it was built to be trusted
People approve. Every AI claim shows its source. AI notes are labeled and can be deleted in one click. Customer data stayed separate across about 100,000 linked records. Some work moved from AI back to plain code where it had to be exact.

### Sales pipeline
A pipeline that researches each prospect, drafts outreach in the salesperson's voice, tracks every lead's stage, and flags who's due for a follow-up each morning. A person reads and sends every message.

### Research
A research helper that gathers the sources, pulls out the facts that matter, and writes a short brief where every claim links back to where it came from.

### Marketing run by AI agents
Agents plan the calendar, draft posts and emails in the brand's voice, learn from the owner's edits, and report what performed. Nothing goes out without a person's OK.

No names, no numbers, and no results on these three. The "Beyond the shop" heading is gone.
